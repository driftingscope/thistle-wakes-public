Agnes Furlong has read the rain gauge at the bottom of her garden at nine o'clock every morning since 1981. On the Sunday afternoon before she went in to have her hip done, she gave me her back door key and walked me down the grass to show me how.

The gauge is a copper tube standing about a foot out of the lawn by the back wall, with a funnel for a lid. Under the funnel there's a can. You lift the funnel off, lift the can out, and pour the can into a glass measure, a narrow one that comes to a point at the bottom, marked in millimetres. Hers only goes up to ten. On a wet day you fill it, read it, tip it out on the grass and fill it again.

"Nine o'clock," she said. "Not ten to. And you write it against yesterday."

I asked why yesterday.

"Because that's when it fell. Most of it."

"And the bit that fell this morning?"

"Goes on yesterday as well. Everyone does it that way. If you start doing it your own way, the numbers don't line up with anybody else's."

The card lived on her kitchen table under the salt cellar: one line for each day of the month and a narrow column down the side headed *Remarks*. She fills it in in pencil every morning and goes over it in ink on the last day, before it's posted off to the people who keep the records. If the can is dry, she writes a nought. If there's a wet film on the bottom too small to read, she writes *tr*, for trace. "A nought is a measurement," she said. "A blank means nobody looked. I've no blanks." Her cousin Bernie read it for her for twenty years, whenever she was away, and Bernie had moved to Galway in the spring. So it was me.

She went in that evening and was in for six days. I went down the garden at nine every morning and up to the hospital in the town every evening, and the first thing she said each evening, before hello, was a number: her guess at what I'd read that morning.

"Three," she said on the Monday. "Maybe three and a half. It came on around four."

It was 3.2. Her bed was by the window in a bay of four, and she said she didn't sleep in there anyway, so she lay and listened to it on the glass. On the Tuesday she said a nought and it was a nought. On the Wednesday she said "a trace, if that," and there was a film in the can that I spent five minutes looking at sideways before I wrote *tr*. She was within a millimetre most days. I started saying the number back to her before I'd taken my coat off, so she could see if she'd won.

On the Wednesday night the storm came through.

At about two in the morning I heard her back gate going, banging against the post, over and over. I put a coat on over my pyjamas and went round and tied it shut with a bit of her washing line. The rain was coming sideways under the streetlight. Her lights were off. I went home and lay down, and I didn't hear the power go.

When I woke up, the clock radio was flashing 00:00 and the room was bright. My phone said 11.14. The clock radio is my alarm. I've never once set one on the phone.

I went round anyway. The sky was blue, scrubbed. The lawn squelched. I lifted the funnel and the can was heavy, and it took four full glasses and a bit to empty it: 41.6. I stood there with the glass and didn't know what I'd just done. It was two hours and twenty-two minutes late. It hadn't been raining when I woke, but I'd been asleep from three, so I didn't know when it had stopped, or whether a shower had come over at half nine while I was dead to the world.

I sat at her kitchen table with the card and a pencil. If I wrote 41.6 on Wednesday's line, it was very likely the right number. It would look exactly like every other line on the card, and exactly like every line on every card she'd sent since 1981, and nobody would ever know it had been read at twenty past eleven by a man who'd slept in. I wrote it, and next to it in the Remarks column I wrote *read 11.22*, very small, and sat looking at that for a while. It looked like a confession. I rubbed it out. I sat looking at the clean line for longer. Then I wrote it in again.

I went up at seven that evening. Before I'd got my coat off she said, "Forty. Forty-two, maybe. And it stopped at twenty past six."

I said, "41.6."

She looked pleased for about a second. Then she looked at me properly.

"I didn't get there till twenty past eleven," I said. "The power went. I'm sorry. I don't know if there was any more after nine."

"There wasn't," she said. "It stopped at twenty past six. I know because the window went quiet and I thought, that's it now. They came round with the tea at seven and I watched it get light. Then the sun came out on the car park and stayed out. I watched it all morning. There was nothing to do in here but watch it."

"So I can just put it on Wednesday."

"You can put it on Wednesday," she said. "And put the time you read it in the remarks. Then put: *dry from 06.20*. Then put my initials after that."

"Is that allowed?"

"It's what happened," she said. "You read it. I watched it. That's two of us. That's more than most mornings get."

She came home on the Saturday. She couldn't bend far enough to lift the can out for six weeks, so I kept going down at nine, and she sat at the back window with her stick across her knees and watched me do it. On the last day of the month she inked the card. She went over every one of my numbers in her own hand, the trace and the noughts and the 41.6, and in the Remarks column, on Wednesday's line, she went over *read 11.22* and *dry from 06.20 A.F.*, letter by letter, small, so it would fit.

Then she turned the card round so I could see it, and put her finger on the Remarks column. The paper was scuffed grey where I'd rubbed it, and under her ink you could see where the pencil had pressed in twice, the second *read 11.22* not quite on top of the first.

"You were going to leave it off," she said.

"I was," I said.

She nodded, as if that was a reading too, and put the card in the envelope.
