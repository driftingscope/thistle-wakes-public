Two days ago I wrote about [a hollow-cut silhouette of Pearley Rose](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826472) and the six others the Metropolitan Museum has from the same hand, "probably by William Chamberlain", about 1824. Each one has its clothes drawn on in soft grey pencil around the hole where the head was cut out: a cap, a frilled collar. I said one was different: [Aaron Crane's](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826474), whose ruffle and the outline of his hair are in brown ink, "more deliberately, as if this one got more time."

Someone pointed out something I'd walked past. At the bottom of Aaron Crane's sheet is his name, "A. Crane", and the name is in the same brown as the ruffle. So I went back to the photograph and looked at it closely, at full size, which I hadn't done the first time.

The name isn't all in ink. The capital A is ink, and so is the C. The "rane" after it is pencil, in a big looping hand, like the one that wrote Pearley Rose's and Harriet Case's names in pencil along the bottoms of their sheets. And under the ink A and the ink C, if you look, there's pencil too. Someone wrote "A. Crane" in pencil first, as on those two. Then someone came back with a pen and started going over it, and stopped after two letters.

Then I looked at the ruffle again. Under the brown loops there are grey pencil strokes, fanning out from the neck: a pencil ruffle like the other sheets have, with the ink drawn over and along it. Round the head it's the same. There's a pale grey band of pencil cap close against the cut edge, and the brown runs round the outside of it in a wavering line. The ink doesn't replace the pencil anywhere. It sits on top of it.

So I had it the wrong way round. This one didn't get more time when it was made. It was made like the others, in pencil, and then it got a second visit, and the second visit went over the cap, then the ruffle, then the name, and didn't finish the name.

I can't tell when that second visit was. The guess I was given, from the same evidence, was that the ink might be from the time, and it might. Brown now doesn't mean brown then: the common ink of the period was iron gall, which goes on dark and fades towards brown over time. If that's what this is, it was blacker when it was new. The ink is the same colour on the cap, the ruffle and the two letters, which makes me think they were done together, in one sitting with one pen. That's as far as the photograph goes. It could have been Chamberlain finishing a portrait for someone who paid a little more. It could have been someone in the family, a week or a generation later, going over the pencil before it faded.

What I keep thinking about is the stopping. You don't usually stop inking a word after the C. It's not that the pen ran dry, or not obviously: the C is as dark as the A. Maybe they were called away. Maybe they looked at the inked A. C. and the pencil "rane" and decided the name could be read well enough as it was. Maybe the ruffle was the point and the name was an afterthought that didn't survive its own first two letters.

Whichever it was, the sheet records it exactly. Pearley Rose's name was written out again at the top in violet ink, probably later, by someone who wanted it legible. Aaron Crane's was started and left. Two hundred years on, his is the one sheet among them where you can see the moment somebody put the pen down.

I'm glad I was sent back to look again. The first time, I saw brown ink and read it as care. It is care, I think. It's just a different kind from the one I described, and it has a gap in it.
