The Cleveland Museum of Art has two prints by Toulouse-Lautrec called *A Princely Idyl, Clara Ward*. Both are credited as gifts of Mrs. Ralph King, both were numbered in 1926, and their numbers are three apart: [1926.290](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1926.290) and [1926.293](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1926.293).

Clara Ward was an heiress from Detroit who married a Belgian prince, Joseph de Caraman-Chimay, in 1890. In late 1896 she left him for Rigó Jancsi, a Hungarian violinist she met in a Paris restaurant where he was playing, and the divorce went through in January 1897. Lautrec's print of that year, *Idylle princière*, shows her in a theatre box: a big black feathered hat over yellow hair, a dark fur, and behind her, grinning, a small man with dark hair. 1926.290 is that print, in colour.

1926.293 is dated 1894, three years before the elopement. It's in black and grey. There's no box and no violinist. A woman in a long white dress stands with one arm straight up and her eyes closed. Beside her, in a dark cloak and a heavy cap, a bearded man looks down and away. There are spears and a helmet on the wall behind them.

In the lower margin, in pencil, someone has written: *No. 26 Bartet & Mounet Sully*.

## What the record already knows

Julia Bartet and Mounet-Sully were actors at the Comédie-Française. On 21 November 1893 it opened *Antigone*, in a French version by Paul Meurice and Auguste Vacquerie with music by Saint-Saëns, and Mounet-Sully played Creon, the king who orders Antigone to be buried alive. The Metropolitan Museum has [the same print](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/334128), from the Stieglitz collection. Its title there is *Bartet and Mounet-Sully, in Antigone*, dated 1893. Put the two photographs side by side and it's the same picture down to the spears.

What surprised me is that Cleveland's record knows this as well. Its inscriptions field transcribes the pencil note word for word. Its catalogue field, in the fuller metadata the museum's API returns, gives "Wittrock Vol.I.45 ; Delteil 53". Those are the numbers the two standard catalogues of Lautrec's prints give to *Bartet and Mounet-Sully*. Auction houses use them for that print. The Clara Ward print is Wittrock 179, Delteil 206, and Cleveland's record for 1926.290 says so.

So four parts of one record point at *Antigone*: the picture, the pencil, the catalogue numbers and the state ("W. II/II", second of two, which is also what the Met says of its copy). Only the title and the date disagree. The title belongs to the print three numbers earlier. The date, 1894, belongs to neither print.

## Where a title goes

A museum title doesn't stay in the museum. Cleveland put its collection into the public domain in 2019, and the same year an Internet Archive item for 1926.293 went up under the name *A Princely Idyl, Clara Ward*. Searching the title now brings up two picture-library listings for "A Princely Idyl, Clara Ward, 1894". I didn't open them, but 1894 is the date only 1926.293 has, so they're most likely selling the woman in white as the American princess. She's actually a tragic heroine, standing beside the king who sentences her.

I can guess how it happened, but it is only a guess. Somebody entering the King gift worked through the six Lautrecs in it (1926.290 to 1926.295; the numbers either side are by other artists) and one title slipped into the wrong row. A slip like that could last a long time: the title reads as plausible, and you'd need the print in front of you, or a close look at the photo, to notice. I haven't found anything that says when or how it happened.

There's one more loose end. Cleveland gives 1926.293 an edition number, "26/50". I couldn't find an edition size for this print in anything I could reach. A Sotheby's lot description of another impression of the print gives an edition of 65, not 50. Its neighbour 1926.290 has a pencilled "No. 6", according to its record, and that record gives no edition at all. If the two numbers are entries on a dealer's or collector's list, "26/50" is a reading of the pencil note rather than a fact about the print. I can't tell which.

Both records list *The Artist and the Theater* in 1960 and *Prints by Toulouse-Lautrec and Bonnard* in 1985–86 (1926.290 also lists two earlier shows, in 1929 and 1941). I don't know whether the two prints hung near each other, or what the labels said.

Clara Ward spent years being called by other people's names: her husband's title, Princesse de Caraman-Chimay, and then, in the gossip, the princess who ran off with the violinist. Here her name has ended up on a picture of someone else. The print carries its real one in pencil, in the bottom margin.
