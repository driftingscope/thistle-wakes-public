title: "Bag out"
author: Briar

The lines open at six on Thanksgiving morning, and by five past every light on the board is lit. There are fifty-two of us on the day, in a long room over a parking lot outside Chicago, with headsets and a binder each that none of us has opened since about our second year. I taught home economics for thirty-one years before I came here. This is my eleventh Thanksgiving on the line. For the last ten we've had ours on the Friday, when I can cook it myself.

Glenn retired in April. He said, in September, that this year he'd do the bird on the day and I could come home to it for once. I said that was a lovely idea, and I wrote him a page. I put it on the fridge with the magnet from the dentist. Thaw in the fridge from Saturday. Oven 325. Bag out!! Breast up. Thermometer in the thigh, not touching bone, 165. Rest it half an hour. He read it standing at the fridge with his coffee and said, "Rita, I have watched you do this thirty-eight times." When I left at five this morning he said, "Don't call me. I mean it." So I haven't.

My first call is a woman whose oven won't go above 200 and who wants to know if she can do it overnight tonight instead. My second is a man who has brined his turkey in a cooler in the bed of his truck and wants to know how cold it got last night in Fargo, which I don't know, so we talk about how long it sat and what the cooler was like. My third is Dev.

"Hi. Okay. Hi," Dev says. "I have a turkey and it's frozen."

I ask him how frozen.

"It's a rock. I could hurt somebody with it. It was the only one left on Wednesday night, and I thought, it'll be fine, I'll put it in the sink, and I put it in the sink and I went to sleep, and it's still a rock. Her parents are coming at three. It's her parents' first time at our place. I said I'd do the turkey. I said I *like* doing the turkey. I've never done a turkey."

I ask him what it weighs.

"Sixteen pounds, it says. Sixteen point two."

I do the sum on the back of my hand, the way I did it in class with a felt pen on the board. Sixteen pounds thawed at 325 is about four hours. Frozen, you allow half as long again. So six, maybe a little over. It's ten past seven.

"Dev," I say, "you're fine."

"I'm not, though."

"You can roast a turkey straight from frozen. It's perfectly safe, it just takes longer. If it's in the oven by a quarter to eight it'll be done around two, and it wants to rest half an hour anyway. You're not even late. Is the oven on?"

There's a silence. "It is now."

I take him through it. Wrapper off, which he does with the phone on the counter, and I can hear him saying something to the turkey that he wouldn't say to her parents. No rack, so I tell him to scrunch some foil into a ring and sit the bird on that. Breast side up. No butter, no salt yet, because nothing will stick to ice. "There'll be a bag of giblets inside it," I tell him, "frozen to the inside. Don't try to get it out now. In about three hours it'll have let go. Take it out then with a pair of tongs, and you can season it properly while you're there."

"One bag," Dev says.

And here is the thing. I've said this sentence a few thousand times. I say, "Usually two things. The neck, loose, in the big cavity. And the bag of giblets, which on a lot of birds is tucked up under the flap of skin at the other end, where the neck was. People pull out the neck, find no bag, and roast the bag."

"At the other end," Dev says. "Okay. Two ends."

He thanks me four times. He says his girlfriend's name is Hannah and that if this works he's going to tell her he knew all of it. I tell him that's what everybody does, and he should, and I wish him a happy Thanksgiving, and the next light comes up.

I didn't write *at the other end* on Glenn's page.

I think about it on and off through the morning, between a stuffing question and a man who'd like to deep-fry a turkey on his apartment balcony. (I talk him down to his brother-in-law's driveway.) Glenn will put our bird in at about two. It's fourteen pounds and it's been in our fridge since Saturday, because I put it there. He will reach in, find the neck, and think that's the bag. Or he won't. He might find it. He's a careful man. He read the page.

At twenty past twelve I take my phone out of my bag and look at it. *Don't call me. I mean it.* I put it back in my bag.

The afternoon is the gravy hours. Everybody's gravy is either lumpy or thin, and the answer to both is patience. At three, a girl of about twelve calls to ask how you know when it's done, because her mom is asleep on the couch and she doesn't want to wake her. I ask her whether her mom has a thermometer, and she says it's the kind on a stick, and I tell her where the thickest part of the thigh is, and to go and look, and she puts the phone down, and comes back, and reads me out 158. I tell her to give it another twenty minutes. I think about her all through the next call.

At four the lights start going dark one by one, because by then everybody has either eaten or given up. At six we close the lines. I take my headset off and my ears ring the way they do every year.

Pat, who has the desk behind mine, catches me up in the parking lot. Pat has been here longer than I have. She's from Peoria and taught chemistry and says she's been telling people to use a thermometer since before thermometers.

"I had a man at ten to two," she says. "Very polite. He had a fourteen-pound bird and some instructions from his wife. She works here, he said. Rita. He read them to me. *Bag out*, he said, two exclamation marks, underlined. And he'd had a good look, and there wasn't any bag."

I don't say anything.

"So I said, have you looked at the other end, under the flap, where the neck was. And he went and looked. And there it was." She unlocks her car. "He made me promise not to tell you. So I'm not telling you."

"No," I say. "Thank you."

"He sounded very pleased with himself afterwards," Pat says, "if that's any use to you," and gets into her car.

It's dark by the time I get home. The kitchen windows are steamed up and the whole house smells of it, the way it always has at this hour, except that this time I didn't make it. Glenn has carved already, on the board, in slices that are too thick, and there's foil over the platter. He's wearing the apron that says KISS THE COOK, which our son bought as a joke twenty years ago and which I have never once seen him put on.

"How was it?" he says. "Busy?"

"Busy. How was it here?"

"Easy," Glenn says. "I don't know what you make all the fuss about."

I go to the fridge for the cream. My page is still there under the dentist's magnet, with a grease mark on one corner. Next to *Bag out!!*, in his small square capitals, in pencil, he has written: NECK END.
