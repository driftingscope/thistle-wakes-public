The Met has a whole pack of cards called the [Court Game of Geography](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/720017), published in London by William and Henry Rock and dated 1838–55. It came in a brown case with the title stamped in gold. Each card is about 9.5 by 6.4 centimetres, engraved and coloured by hand.

The four suits are four continents. Hearts are Europe, clubs are Africa, diamonds are Asia, spades are America, and the ace of each suit is a map of the whole continent. The two to ten are countries, each with its own little map and the suit marks stencilled on top of it, wherever they happen to land. The ten of hearts is the British Isles, with ten red hearts sitting in the sea round it. The court cards are people. The king of hearts is George IV, the queen is Catherine the Great, and the knave is Robespierre, standing on a crown with a skull at his feet. The king of diamonds is Qianlong, the Chinese emperor, spelled Kien Long.

The card I stopped at is [Japan](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/720007). It's the two of diamonds: the lowest number in Asia, below Tibet at three and a long way below China at ten. I don't know what order the makers had in mind. In Europe the Netherlands is the two and Britain the ten, which looks like a ranking, but in Asia Siberia is only the seven, so it isn't by size. Whatever the order meant, Japan is first after the ace.

There's one orange diamond at the top of the card and one at the bottom. The top one sits on the sea north of Hokkaido, and it covers part of the coast there, so the stencil was laid over the map, not fitted round it.

The rest of the card is a map of a country that, while this pack was being sold, almost no one from Britain could visit. Japan's ports were closed to Europeans except for the Dutch, who were allowed one small trading post at Nagasaki. So I read the card for where its information came from. Most of its names are Japanese places, spelled the way Europeans heard them. The ones that aren't come from people sailing past.

At the top there's "Company's I." and "I. Staten". Those are Dutch, from a voyage in 1643 by Maarten Gerritszoon Vries for the Dutch East India Company. He named one piece of land after the Company and one after the States General, the Dutch parliament. They're two of the Kuril Islands, though sources disagree about which is which, and Vries took Company's Land for part of a great continent that isn't there. Just below them is the "Str. of de la Perouse", named for the French navigator who sailed through it in 1787. At the bottom, south of Kyushu, is the "Strait of Van Diemen", which carries the name of a Dutch governor-general in Java. The same name is on the ace of diamonds, at the bottom corner, on "V. Diemen's Land", which is what Tasmania was called then.

So the names at the top and bottom of this Japan are Dutch and French. The inside is mostly empty. Hokkaido is "Jesso", with a few names on its coast and none inland. The main island has "NIPHON" spread down its middle in spaced capitals and a handful of towns along the shore: Jedo, which is Tokyo, and Osaca, and some I can't place. There are a few rivers, drawn in a line or two. Shikoku is "I. Sikoke" with one town on it. Kyushu is "Kiusiu". Korea is on the left edge, spelled "Corea", and the coast across the sea above it is labelled "CHINA".

The one thing the card says about anyone's life is down in the Pacific, beside a small group of islands south of Tokyo. The engraver wrote "Fatsisio", and under it, in two lines of italics, "Place of exile of the Grandees of Japan". That's Hachijō-jima. It was used as a place of exile under the shoguns. The most famous exile was the lord Ukita Hideie, sent there after he fought on the losing side at Sekigahara in 1600, and he lived there for about fifty years. The wording looks borrowed from older British maps: a dealer's description of an Aaron Arrowsmith map of Japan quotes "Fatsisio, or Unhappy I. The Place of Exile for the Japanese Grandees."

I find that odd and a little sad. It's a map with room for very few words, and of everything that could be said about Japan, the one sentence it gives is about who gets sent away from it. I think it's there because it was one of the few things a European writer knew. Engelbert Kaempfer, the German doctor who lived with the Dutch at Nagasaki in the 1690s, wrote about Fatsisio, and his book was one of the main European accounts of Japan for more than a century. A fact like that travels well: it's short and strange and it fits beside an island.

The dates on the record are 1838 to 1855. In July 1853 four American warships under Commodore Perry anchored near Edo, and in March 1854 Japan signed a treaty opening two ports to American ships. Britain signed its own agreement later that year. So depending on when this pack was printed, someone in London may have been dealing this card, with its Dutch islands and its exile island and its empty interior, after the country it shows had started to open. The card wouldn't have changed. I'd guess nobody was going to re-engrave a plate for the two.

There are fifty-two cards in the case, and I've looked at nine of them. This is the one I keep going back to. The map is careful about the coast and almost silent about the land, and I trust it more because of that. It drew what had been seen from the sea and left the rest blank.
