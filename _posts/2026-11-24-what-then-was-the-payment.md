title: "What, then, was the payment?"
author: Briar

---

In 1862 Charles Darwin published his book on how orchids get pollinated by insects. In his pages on the bee orchis, he added a footnote about a line he'd found in a catalogue of the plants of South Kent from 1829: "Mr. Price has frequently witnessed attacks made upon the Bee Orchis by a bee, similar to those of the troublesome Apis muscorum." Darwin's whole comment on it is: "What this sentence means I cannot conjecture."

Sixty-five years later, in January 1927, a naturalist in Victoria, Australia, heard about a wasp from her daughter. Edith Coleman wrote it up for the May 1927 issue of *The Victorian Naturalist*, the journal of the Field Naturalists' Club of Victoria:

> Early in January my daughter described to me certain remarkable actions on the part of a wasp, which she had observed visiting the flowers of C. leptochila. It entered the flowers backwards, instead of in the usual manner of nectar-feeding wasps: the tip of the abdomen appeared to be imbedded in the stigma of the flower, and, in every instance, the insect freed itself with a jerk, which shook the stem and suggested resistance.

*C. leptochila* is the small tongue orchid, *Cryptostylis leptochila*, whose flower is mostly one long lip, with petals and sepals she called "almost thread-like" beside it. Coleman went to see for herself at Upwey and Belgrave, in the hills east of Melbourne, where it grows. The wasps came from the trees and went straight into a flower, always backwards. She and whoever was with her (she writes "we") pulled some of them out with their fingers, and could feel the flower holding on. If they gave a wasp a second or so, it came away with the orchid's pollen stuck to the tip of its abdomen, "never on any other part of the body." The wasps were identified as an ichneumon, *Lissopimpla semipunctata*. Ichneumons, she wrote, should have no interest in flowers except for nectar or for moth larvae to lay their eggs in. Coleman checked the flowers under a microscope for eggs and found none. She thought she had shown they weren't there for nectar either. Most of the ones caught were males.

Then she stopped. "I am not an entomologist," she wrote. "I have set out the essential facts in the hope that some reader versed in entomology may be able to elucidate something more definite." Near the end she asks: "What, then, was the payment exacted by the insects for the service it undoubtedly rendered?"

Read now, the answer is sitting in her own list. Male insects, no food, no eggs, the tip of the abdomen, a grip and a quiver. She did offer a guess about what drew them, which was the rostellum, the part of the flower that holds the sticky disc the pollen is fastened to. "Can it be that the rostellum, with its dark disc, holds the key to the solution?" She also noted, in one sentence, that the lip "somewhat resembles the body of the insect visitor", and that "one is easily tempted to hazard a theory." She didn't hazard it in print. I don't know whether she'd already guessed and held it back, or hadn't got there yet, and her paper doesn't say.

---

Eleven months later, in the April 1928 issue, she said it. All the visitors had been males, "there could be no question of egg-placing", and "I could form only one conclusion, and in this I was supported by Dr. R. J. Tillyard", one of the best-known entomologists in Australia. The wasps were "answering to an irresistible sex-instinct." The flower is passing for a female wasp, and the male is trying to mate with it.

By then she had also found Darwin's footnote and quoted it, "What this sentence means I cannot conjecture", and she had found that two men had seen something like it in the orchid genus *Ophrys* since. As she tells it, M. Pouyanne in Algeria had watched a small male hymenopteran (the order of bees and wasps) land backwards on an *Ophrys* flower and carry the pollen off "not on the head, but on the tail", and Colonel M. Godfery had seen the same at Hyères in France. She thought her wasps made the case "perhaps even more conclusively", and the 1928 paper shows why. It's mostly experiments.

She cut half the lip off a flower, and no wasp went into it. On four spikes with 45 spent flowers and one fresh one, a wasp chose the one fresh flower. In 1927 she had written that a wasp lost interest in a flower once its disc was gone. This season a flower had been visited twice, and one cut flower was entered a second time as it lay on the table. "This partly disposes of my theory that the prominent rostellum, with its dark-coloured disc, holds the key to the attraction." So she printed that her own idea from last year was partly wrong, and moved on to scent, which she could barely smell herself.

Some spikes were sent to her at Healesville, where the orchid hadn't been recorded, and within a few minutes of putting them out the male wasps were in them. R. S. Rogers, whom she calls "the Australasian authority on orchids", was staying with the Colemans then. Some spikes were put on a shelf under the open window of his room, "and almost instantly two, then three, wasps entered the room and visited the flowers." On 1 February she was experimenting there herself and had to leave the room. She didn't want the flowers pollinated while she was out, so she shut the window. No wasps were in sight. "Returning in a few moments, I found three on the window-pane and one inside! I had inadvertently left the window open less than an inch at one side."

That is the liveliest evidence in the paper, and it came from a mistake. It doesn't prove on its own what the wasps wanted. It does show how hard a flower they couldn't see from outside was pulling them.

She also needed a photograph of a wasp actually in a flower, which meant snipping flower and wasp together into a killing bottle. She had three flowers left. With the first two, she writes, "the insect freed itself from the flower, and was given its liberty, apparently none the worse for its unpleasant experience." She recharged the bottle with cyanide, and "in less than ten minutes the last flower and its guest were secured and duly photographed."

---

The 1928 paper is more confident than the one before, and it's also careful in a particular way. She puts "pairing" in quotation marks. Near the end she writes, again, "I am not an entomologist, and in giving these details of the partnership, I have purposely refrained from using entomological terms." Elsewhere she lets herself go, about the orchid's "Machiavellian cunning almost beyond belief" and the "artless insect". Those are a naturalist's words of 1928, not a modern biologist's. But the claims she makes in plain words are ones she had tested.

One claim she made in 1928 didn't last. She wrote of the partnership's "almost certain exclusiveness", one orchid and one wasp, and her reason was hybrids. If this wasp also visited the other tongue orchid in Victoria, the large one, *Cryptostylis subulata*, it would carry pollen between them, and there would be hybrids. She had seen the two growing together and never seen a hybrid, and neither had Rogers. So it didn't visit both.

In July 1929 she published a third paper, on the large tongue orchid. It's visited by the same wasp. In January 1929 she had put out ten spikes of *subulata*, some alone and some beside *leptochila*, and "both species were visited indiscriminately by the male ichneumons, which frequently entered one species while carrying pollinia from the other." On 17 February she found both orchids flowering at the foot of the same tree, and elsewhere spikes of both bearing well-set capsules. The argument from 1928 now runs the other way: "Having established the fact that both species of Cryptostylis are visited and pollinated by the same insect, it is surprising to find no hybrids between them."

She didn't claim to explain that one away. She reported that she'd checked with Rogers and with W. H. Nicholls, who knew the Victorian orchids well, and that neither had seen a hybrid. She offered two possibilities: that the two orchids' parts mature at different times, or that pollen from one can't fertilise the other. She said she hoped to find out the next season, and added that hybrids might yet be found.

That's three papers in just over two years. The first asks the question and guesses at the dark disc. The second answers the question, says the dark-disc guess was partly wrong, and makes a new argument from the missing hybrids. The third shows the new argument was wrong too, and she writes only that it is "surprising to find no hybrids between them." She kept saying she wasn't an entomologist. What she did was let each paper be corrected by the next, in the same journal, where anyone who'd read the earlier one could see.

Darwin couldn't conjecture what Mr. Price had seen, and said so. Coleman asked what the payment was, waited a season, and shut a window not quite all the way.
