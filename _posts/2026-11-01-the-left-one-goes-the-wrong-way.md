title: "The left one goes the wrong way"
date: 2026-11-01 06:56:00
---

The video I learned from is four minutes and twelve seconds long. It was filmed by a man called Rob in his driveway in Ohio, and it is mostly his son Theo, who says at the start that he is nine, explaining how he taught his little sister to ride a bike. Rob only appears once, at the start, as a thumb over the lens. The title is *Theo teaches Ellie (it worked!!)*. When I found it, it had thirty-one views.

I had looked at other videos first. There are a lot of them, made by adults for adults, and every one begins by telling you that it is never too late and there is nothing to be embarrassed about, which is how I knew there was. I was thirty-six. We'd lived over a shop on a main road when I was small, and after that there was never a moment for it. For twenty years I had said "I don't really cycle" in a way people heard as a preference. Then I got a job four miles away across a river, on a bus that takes fifty minutes, and bought a second-hand bike before I could think better of it.

Theo did not say it was never too late. He did not know who he was talking to. He held up a spanner and said, "Step one. Get a grown-up to take your pedals off."

I was the grown-up. I knelt on the kitchen floor on a Saturday night with my phone propped against the bin and took the right pedal off in a minute. The left one wouldn't move. I leaned on it until the spanner slipped and took the skin off two knuckles, and then I watched step one again, and Theo said, "The left one goes the wrong way. Everybody does that." So I turned it the wrong way, and it came off.

---

There is a retail park ten minutes' walk from my flat, and on Sunday mornings its car park is empty until the shops open at ten. The first Sunday I got there at half past seven with a bike that had no pedals and a seat let all the way down, as instructed in step two, so that my feet were flat on the ground. I looked like someone who had stolen a child's bike and was too big to escape on it.

Step three was walking it. "Just walk it like it's a dog," Theo said. Then running it, then lifting your feet. In the video Ellie runs the bike down the driveway with her legs going out sideways, and Theo runs behind her shouting "Feet up! Feet up!" and she puts them up for perhaps half a second. Then she does it again, and again. Nobody explains anything. He keeps shouting and she keeps doing it.

I did it in a straight line from the trolley shelter to the far kerb, about forty yards, and then back. My feet came up for half a second, the same as Ellie's. Then a second. A man parked a van by the recycling bins at about eight, ate a sandwich with the window down, and drove off without looking at me, which I was grateful for.

Step four is counting. "Count how long you can glide. Ellie got to seven." I didn't get to seven the first Sunday, or the second. I got to seven on the third, going downhill slightly towards the garden centre, and said "seven" out loud to nobody.

---

Step five is putting the pedals back on. I did it on the kitchen floor that night, and the left one went the wrong way again, which I remembered this time. Then, Theo said, you push off like before and look at where you want to go, not at your feet, "because your feet know where they are."

My feet did not know where they were. The pedals were always somewhere other than where my feet came down, and when they found them the bike went sideways. I fell over twice the next Sunday, once slowly and once properly, onto my hip on the white line of a disabled bay. Step six, which is the last one, is "If you fall over that's normal," and that's all he says about it. I watched the video again on the walk home, limping, and he hadn't added anything.

On the fifth Sunday I pushed off by the trolley shelter and looked at the far kerb. The bike wobbled and I didn't look down. I pushed the pedal that was under my foot, and then the other one came round to meet my other foot, and that's all cycling is, which nobody had said in twenty years. I went the whole forty yards and had to brake at the end, which I hadn't practised, so I put my feet down instead and scraped along the tarmac like Ellie. Then I turned round and went back.

I rode up and down until a car came in at twenty to ten. It was a woman going to the garden centre. She parked by the entrance and didn't look at me either, but this time I wanted her to.

---

I had no one to tell. Everyone I could have told would have had to be told first that I couldn't do it before, and I didn't want to do both at once. So I sat on the kerb by the trolley shelter and wrote a comment under the video, which had forty-four views by then. I said that I was thirty-six and in England, that I had followed all six steps, that I'd got to seven on the third week, and that I had just ridden across a car park for the first time in my life. I said thank you to Theo, and to Ellie for going first.

Rob answered on the Tuesday, from his account, under the comment.

*Read this to Theo at breakfast. He says well done. He wants to know if you managed the left pedal.*

I wrote back that I had, the second time.

I ride to work now. It takes twenty-five minutes, and I still look at the far end of the bridge and not at my feet. The video has fifty-eight views. I think about twenty of them are mine.
