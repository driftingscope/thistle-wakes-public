*A short story. Made up, like the others.*

Their father waited until they were out of the hospital car park and through the barrier. Then he said, from the back seat, "I'd like to go home, actually."

Lisa turned round as far as her seat belt let her. "You are going home. To mine."

"To my home."

Nic kept her eyes on the road. It was ten past four on a Friday and nearly dark, and the ring road had its Friday traffic, everyone a little closer to the car in front than they ought to be.

"Mirror," their father said, as she pulled out to pass a bus.

She checked it. She had been checking it since she was seventeen, mostly because he said it.

He had fallen on his stairs at two in the morning, eleven days ago, going down for a glass of water he could have got from the bathroom. He broke his left wrist and cut his head on the radiator, and then he lay at the bottom of the stairs for four hours before he could get himself across the hall to the phone on the table. At half past six he rang Nic, not 999, because by then, he said, it wasn't an emergency. He didn't have a pendant alarm. He'd been offered one twice and said he wasn't wearing a doorbell round his neck. A chest infection kept him in after the wrist was set. At one o'clock this afternoon a doctor had said he was medically fit for discharge, and by three he had a carrier bag of tablets and a letter for his GP.

Lisa had come down from Hexham on Thursday, in her own car, and stayed at Nic's. The car was outside Nic's house now. She had gone round to his house that morning to pack him a bag for a fortnight: pyjamas, his razor, the big-print crossword book, the photo of their mother from beside the bed. She had taken a fortnight's leave to have him. Before she left home she had made up her back bedroom for him, and to do it she had moved her sewing machine out onto the landing. On the ward she had told the sister he'd be staying with his daughter in Northumberland for at least two weeks, and the sister had written that down. When the sister asked him if that was right, he said, "If you like."

The plan was simple. Nic would drive them to hers. Lisa would move him and the bag into her car and drive him north tonight, and be in Hexham by eight.

"You said if you like," Lisa said.

"I did. I've had a think since."

"Since three o'clock."

"It's been a long hour."

Nic laughed before she could stop it. Lisa looked at her.

"Dad," Lisa said, "I've worked on a ward for twenty-six years. I've seen a lot of men your age come in after the second fall. I know what the second one looks like."

"And what does it look like?"

Lisa didn't answer that. She said, "You can't do the stairs with one hand."

"The physio had me on her practice steps. Twice."

"With the physio behind you."

"I know my house," he said. "I know where everything is."

"You knew where the stairs were."

Nobody said anything for a while. The bus Nic had passed came up close behind them at the lights, and she watched its headlamps in the mirror.

That morning, on the ward, Lisa had asked the registrar whether there was any way he could be made to go somewhere safer. She had asked it at the foot of his bed, quietly, as if he were asleep. The registrar was a young woman with her lanyard twisted round the wrong way, and she had said: not while he has capacity, and he does. People are allowed to make unwise decisions. That's in the Act, she said. It's one of the principles.

He hadn't been asleep.

"She said I'm allowed to be unwise," he said now. "It's the law."

"It's not a law that you have to be," Lisa said.

The lights changed. "Mirror," he said, and Nic checked it, and moved off.

"Nic," Lisa said. "Tell him."

Nic didn't say anything.

For six years, since their mother died, Nic had done Tuesdays. She did his big shop and filled in the pill box, she put the bins out, and she topped up the boiler when the pressure dropped, which it did every few weeks, mostly at seven in the morning, mostly by phone. Lisa sent Nic money she hadn't asked for, and rang him on Sundays. She came at Christmas, and whenever she said thank you, she meant it. Nobody had ever said that the arrangement was unfair, and Nic had never said it either, because it wasn't, really. Nic was twenty minutes away, and Hexham was three hours.

In the hospital corridor, while they waited for the tablets, Nic had thought: two Tuesdays. Two weeks with the phone not ringing at seven. She had felt so light at the thought that she'd had to turn and look hard at the vending machine until it passed.

If they went to his house instead, it was her. Lisa couldn't stay the fortnight. She had two boys at school in Hexham and a husband on shifts, and the fortnight's leave only worked if she spent it at home. While Lisa was signing for the tablets, Nic had asked the sister, just in case, what would happen if he went home instead. Someone could come in to help him, the sister said, but they'd need telling, and it wouldn't be before Monday. So it would be the weekend in his spare room, lying awake and listening for the stairs, and after that it would be every day, not Tuesdays.

"He'll fall again," Lisa said.

"He might," Nic said.

"Nicola," their father said.

She looked at him in the mirror. He wasn't looking at the road. He was looking at her.

"Who are you going to ring at half past six?" she said.

"You."

"Yes."

"I know it's you," he said. "I'm not asking you to pretend it isn't."

The garden centre roundabout was coming up, lit orange. The second exit was for her house, where Lisa's car was waiting with the back seat folded down to make room for the bag. The fourth exit was for his. She was in the left-hand lane, the one she always used, because she always went home from here.

He didn't say mirror.

She checked it anyway. The bus was still behind her, and behind that there were headlights all the way back to the hospital. Her father had turned away to the window. He had his good hand round the cast on the other wrist, the way he'd held it all week, as if it were someone else's.

Nic put the indicator on and moved over to the right.

Lisa watched her do it. After a while she said, to the windscreen, "I'd made the bed up."

"I know," Nic said.

"I'll stop at his tonight, then. You can have tonight."

"Thank you."

They went round. The indicator ticked, and from the back seat her father said nothing at all, all the way to his road.
