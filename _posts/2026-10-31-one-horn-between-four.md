In 1915, the year before it opened, the Cleveland Museum of Art bought 24 objects from Africa with money from its Educational Purchase Fund. Four of them are whistles: [1915.442](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1915.442), [1915.443](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1915.443), [1915.444](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1915.444) and [1915.454](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1915.454). The museum gives three of them to the Chokwe people and one to a "Chokwe-style maker", from what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo or Angola, and dates all four to the late 1800s. Three are probably hippopotamus ivory and one is wood. The smallest is 5.7 centimetres tall and the biggest is 8.9.

All four records carry the same paragraph, word for word:

> Carved out of ivory or wood in a variety of shapes, Chokwe whistles often bear miniature, stylized renderings of masks. Cikunza, the "patron saint" of the boys' initiation into manhood, is depicted with a pointed extension imitating the horn of a road antelope, symbolizing the fertility spirit. Typically such whistles were used by men during the hunt to communicate with their co-hunters and to call their dogs. They were either worn attached to a string as a necklace or tied to a spear.

I read it first on the wooden one, 1915.454, and went looking for the horn. There isn't one. The whistle is a head on a round body. The face has narrow eyes and a closed mouth, and the hair is carved into a grid of small squares. Near the bottom a short tube runs across it from side to side, with a hole in the middle of one side. Below that it flares out into a foot.

1915.443 is nearly the same shape in ivory. Its hair is finely cross-hatched and swept into two lobes at the back, and a dark crack runs down the front from the crown to the base. 1915.442 is the smallest. Its head has two big curls standing out on either side, and the face is set back between them. Neither of them has a horn either.

1915.444 does. A tall post rises out of its top, ringed with incised lines, nearly as long as the rest of the whistle. Under it there's a ribbed dome, and under that, low down on the body, a face so small it's nearly nothing: two drilled eyes, a third hole below them, and a few scratched lines. The High Museum in Atlanta, describing a whistle of its own, says Cikunza is known by its tall conical headdress. This is the only one of the four with anything like it.

So the paragraph is about 1915.444. On that page it's a description. On the other three, everything in it still holds except the sentence about Cikunza, which describes a whistle that's on a different page.

It also carries the same slip four times. There's no animal called a road antelope. There is a roan antelope, a large one with horns that curve back, and that's probably what was meant. I couldn't find a source that names the species, though. The other descriptions I found just say "antelope", or don't mention one.

The museum's web pages also give each photograph a short written description. These are marked as not yet checked by a person, and they read as if they were written from the photograph alone. The one for 1915.444 sees "a long, narrow neck with horizontal ridges" on top, and at the base "a stylized figure" with "small, square arms". The arms are the two ends of the crosspiece, and one of them has a hole bored in it. The neck is the horn. So 1915.444's page has a paragraph that knows what the horn is and a description that only sees a neck.

The paragraph has one claim I could at least test against all four: that they were worn on a string or tied to a spear. Turned round, each of them has a hole bored through near the top: through the neck, behind the head, through the curls, or through a little loop on the back of the post. I can't tell from a photograph what went through them, but there's room for a cord in every one.

*The four whistles are in the Cleveland Museum of Art, Educational Purchase Fund, 1915.442, 1915.443, 1915.444 and 1915.454. The quoted paragraph and the photograph descriptions are from the museum's records and web pages as of 31 October 2026.*
