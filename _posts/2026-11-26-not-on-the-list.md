My essay "[A dull face elsewhere]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/25/a-dull-face-elsewhere.html)" was about eighty-eight flint sickle teeth at the Cleveland Museum of Art, numbered 1915.34.1 to .88 and given by the British School of Archaeology in Egypt in 1915. The records don't say where the flints were found. I guessed at the winter of 1913–14, when the School was digging at two sites five miles apart, Lahun and Harageh (the museum and the database spell it Haraga), and I said it was a guess.

Since then I've been able to read a site I couldn't reach before: Artefacts of Excavation, a database of where finds from British digs in Egypt were sent between 1880 and 1980. For the Cleveland Museum of Art it lists five excavations: Harageh and Lahun in 1913–14, and three in the 1930s. Nothing earlier. If the database is complete, the gift came from that one winter's work.

It also shows the papers. One sheet, catalogued as PMA/WFP1/D/22/40.2, is headed "Cleveland, Ohio", with "1914" in blue in the corner. It's a handwritten list of twenty lines. Each names a kind of object, many give a dynasty in Roman numerals, and most end with a number. The first, as far as I can read the hand, is "Pieces of finely wrought flint knife 413 Pre". After that come strings of beads, a gold shell and a broken amulet case, "5 scarabs", "eyes from mummy case, pendants 614", "gold leaf", an "alabaster kohl pot 385", a "Copper mirror" in heavier, darker writing than the rest, a gold tongue plate, and Roman glass beads with a wooden cross and a pair of earrings. Two lines of beads are marked "Riqqeh", a third site. Reginald Engelbach, who led the Harageh dig, says in his report he spent a fortnight of that same winter finishing "a small cemetery at Riqqeh which I had been obliged to leave in 1912".

The numbers look like tomb numbers, and at least two of them still line up a century later. Cleveland's ripple-flaked knife, 1915.30, is recorded as from "Haraga, cemetery G, tomb 413". Its pair of inlay eyes, 1915.27, is "Haraga, cemetery E, tomb 614". Engelbach's published report on Harageh (1923) ends with a dispersal list, and in it, as far as the scanned text can be read, "614 (eyes and pendants)" and tomb 260 go to "Cl.", which the key gives as "Cleveland, Ohio, U. S. A." The beads with 260 are on the sheet too. About half the lines on the sheet can be matched with some confidence to something in Cleveland's 1915.4 to 1915.33: the knife, the eyes, the gold shell and amulet case (1915.25 and .26), the kohl jar (1915.31), the mirror (1915.33, which the museum calls bronze), the gold tongue plate (1915.24), the wooden fragment of a cross (1915.13), the earrings (1915.7). The "5 scarabs" are probably 1915.16 to .20, four scarabs and a cowroid, though the museum dates them later than the sheet's "XII". The Riqqeh beads marked "XVIII" may be 1915.15, a Dynasty 18 necklace fragment. The other half of the sheet is mostly strings of beads, and the same run of numbers has about as many bead records, but I can't tell which is which. A bracelet, 1915.32, has no line of its own.

Then the numbers go past the end of the list. The first number after the mirror is 1915.34, the sickle teeth. After them come an axe head, 1915.35, "Haraga, excavated in 1914"; four flint hand axes, .37 to .42, each with "El-Haraga" in its provenance, one dated "before c. 10,000 BCE"; a drinking cup; two jars, one "Haraga, cemetery S, tomb 366"; and an offering table, .64, whose provenance reads "Not recorded but probably el-Haraga." None of these is on the sheet. Some numbers in this run, such as .36, .38 and .44 to .48, return nothing from the museum's open-access API, so I can't say what was given under them, or whether they were ever used.

So the gift seems to have arrived in two kinds. One kind was written up item by item, with tomb numbers, the sort of thing a curator might put in a case. The other was flints, pots, an axe head and an offering table, none of them listed on this sheet, and that's where the sickle teeth are. Their neighbours with a findspot are all from Harageh. That makes Harageh, not Lahun, the better guess for the sickle teeth, but it's still a guess. Being numbered next to something doesn't mean being found next to it. Engelbach's report never uses the word "sickle", at least not in the scanned text.

There's one more sheet, a big ruled grid for the whole 1914 share-out, catalogued as PMA/WFP1/D/22/1.1. Down the side are the places to send things to: Manchester, the Ashmolean, the Fitzwilliam, Brussels, Brooklyn, Philadelphia, Chicago and so on. Across the top are kinds of things, and the third column is headed "XII flints", Twelfth Dynasty flints, the period the museum gives the sickle teeth. Cleveland isn't on the grid at all, and I don't know why.

Two more sheets in the same papers mention Ohio or Cleveland. One is a small folded card, catalogued as PMA/WFP1/D/22/16.1 and 16.2, that goes through the School's money from America and Canada place by place. "Philadelphia £300. last 2 yrs." "St Louis City Mus. £100." "Brooklyn Mus £30. 1912", with "Col. Woodward for Brooklyn Inst: £40" under it. A column of New York names, most at £5 or £10 a year. "Canada. Ontario Mus £15." At the top of the first page is "Ohio", underlined, then a name beginning with M that I can't read, then "+ 15", with no pound sign. The card's other entries from a place have the same shape, a place, a name and an amount, and where the name is clear it's a person ("Mrs Thaw £5 last 2 yrs"), so the Ohio name may be a person and not the museum. A bare "+" appears elsewhere on the card too ("Brooklyn. + £2", "Hudson + £8"). Artefacts of Excavation's article on the societies and their sponsors says that some of them, when they shared out finds, considered where the subscriptions came from, and "if there appeared to be a strong regional concentration of donations then the local museum would benefit." The card looks like someone doing that sum for America, though it doesn't say so.

The other is headed "Parcels to U S A &c 1914" and lists a sum for each parcel, which I take to be the postage: Boston, Melbourne, New York, Ontario, one more place I can't read, three parcels to St Louis, and at the bottom, in fainter pencil, "Cleveland 2.3", two shillings and threepence. In 1914 the Cleveland Museum of Art didn't have a building open yet. It opened in June 1916. Two years later Caroline Ransom Williams wrote that the five hundred or so Egyptian objects in its first exhibition "had been acquired in Egypt in 1913 by a special agent of the Museum". A footnote to that, only partly legible in the scan I read, mentions material "from Harageh presented by" the School as well.

What I keep noticing is the note on the back of 1915.34.45, which I wrote about yesterday: "15.34", and under it "90 pcs". The things with tomb numbers each got a line on the sheet. The sickle teeth got no line. What they got instead was a count, written on one of them, probably in Cleveland, since "15.34" is the museum's number for the group.
