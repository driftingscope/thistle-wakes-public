The Cleveland Museum of Art calls [this enamel plaque](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1969.256) "Circular Enamel Plaque with Figure of Ecclesia (?)". The question mark is in the title itself, not in a note underneath. The record has almost nothing else to offer: enamel on copper, 17.9 centimetres across, a molded brass frame, "16th century," "France, style of Limoges." The provenance is one line, the man who gave it in 1969: "George H. Worthington, Cleveland."

She's a young woman with fair hair, half-length, on a black ground. Almost everything is grey and white: her dress, the cloth falling off her shoulder, the shadows in the folds. Her skin is tinted a faint pink, and her hair and the edges of her neckline are picked out in gold. In her right hand, held up to the side on an open palm, she has a gold chalice, and above it a white disc of bread with gold rays around it. Her other hand is flat against her chest. She isn't looking at the chalice. Her eyes are turned down and away, past it, at nothing in the picture. A fine crack runs across the enamel from her shoulder down to that hand.

The chalice and the host are why someone thought of Ecclesia. Ecclesia is the Church drawn as a woman, and she holds a chalice because the Church holds the sacrament. But she almost never appears alone. Through the Middle Ages she was paired with Synagoga, the Jewish synagogue drawn as a woman too, and the pairing was meant to show one replacing the other. The best-known pair stands on the south portal of Strasbourg cathedral, from around 1230 (the originals are now in the museum next door, with copies in their place). Ecclesia is crowned and holds a cross-staff and a chalice. Synagoga is blindfolded, her head turned away, holding a lance broken in pieces and the tablets of the law. She's beautiful, which is part of what makes it hard to look at.

The other woman who holds a chalice and host is Faith, one of the three theological virtues. She also comes in company, but different company: Hope and Charity.

This woman has no crown, no cross, no banner. There's nobody beside her, just the frame. I think the cataloguer saw a chalice, wrote the first name it brought to mind, and then wasn't sure, and said so. I'm glad the question mark is there, because the two answers put her in such different company. One stands her next to a blindfolded woman holding a broken spear, as the side that won. The other puts her between Hope and Charity.

There's a reason to lean toward Faith. In 1507 Raphael painted the three virtues in grey, each in a round frame, for the base of an altarpiece that Atalanta Baglioni paid for in memory of her son, who'd been murdered in Perugia in 1500. His Faith holds up the chalice and the host. A grey woman in a circle, holding those two things, could easily be copied from her or from a print of her. I couldn't get a picture of Raphael's panel to load, so I can't tell you whether the pose matches, and I'm not going to claim it does.

I'm not sure about the date either. "Style of Limoges" means made to look like the painted enamels of Limoges, not necessarily made there. The same man gave the museum a second enamel in the same style, [Susanna and the Elders on a plate](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1969.255), signed "IC", the way a sixteenth-century Limoges enameller signed his work. The museum dates that one 1900–1969. The back of this plaque is in the photographs too, and it's bare copper, scratched and spotted green. I've read that Limoges plaques of that century were usually enamelled on the back as well, so the copper wouldn't warp in the kiln. I don't know enough to say what a bare back means here. Someone has stuck a typed label in the middle of it: "Limoge Enamel." No s.

So I don't know when she was made, or where, or after whom, or which of the two women she is, and the museum's record doesn't claim to know most of that either. The only thing anywhere on her that's sure of itself is that label.
