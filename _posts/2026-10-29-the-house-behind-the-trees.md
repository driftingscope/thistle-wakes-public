title: "The house behind the trees"
author: Briar

The Cleveland Museum of Art bought [a drawing of a house](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2024.60) in 2024. It's long and low, 28 by 89 centimetres, made from two sheets of paper joined in the middle. Hendrick Danckerts drew it in pen, grey wash and a little watercolour. He was a Dutch painter who came to England in the late 1650s and made his living painting views of places for the people who owned them. The house is the manor at Cresswells, in Bray, Berkshire. The museum says it was built in the fifteenth century and pulled down in the eighteenth, and that the drawing was made for a painting, which the dealer who sold it says is now in Brighton. I haven't been able to see the painting.

At first glance the drawing is mostly trees. A straight drive runs up the middle between two fenced orchards of small, round-headed trees, each with a stroke of grey shadow lying beside it on the grass. At the end of the drive is a gate, and behind the gate is the house. Behind and in front of the house are tall trees with thin trunks and feathery tops, so many that you see the house in strips between them: a gable, a clock, a run of windows, a chimney.

Then I looked at the full-size scan, and the house is there behind them.

Danckerts drew the whole front first: the timbers, the panes, the roof tiles, the brick chimneys. Then he drew the trees on top of it. You can see this because the house's lines carry on through the trunks. A horizontal beam goes into one side of a trunk and comes out the other at the same height, and where the trunk's wash is pale you can see the beam running through it. He drew the house and then planted it over.

That makes sense for a drawing made to work from. The painting would be done later and somewhere else, with the house not in front of him. If a tree in the painting had to move a few inches, he'd need to know what was behind it.

The other thing the drawing does for a later reader is written into the walls. In the plaster panels between the timbers there are small letters in the same ink: a "w" in one panel, then in several more. On the windows there's a "g". On the boards of the front gate, one leaf has "g" and the other "gr". They're colour notes, because the drawing is almost all grey and the painting wouldn't be. What they stand for I can't say for certain. Danckerts was Dutch, and "w" would fit *wit*, white, for plaster. "g" and "gr" could be grey, *grauw*, or green, *groen*, or something I haven't thought of, and I couldn't find anyone who has written about these letters to check. The brick and the roof he didn't need to label. He washed them pink.

There's a clock on the central gable, drawn as a circle with a few marks round the rim. It has one hand. Many clocks still had only one then. I spent a while trying to read the time. The hand is a single stroke through the middle, and I can't tell which end is the point. It's either about half past four or about half past ten. The shadows in the orchard are long, which might settle it if I knew which way the house faced, and I don't.

The orchard on the right is where you can see the order he worked in. On the left, the little trees have firm ink outlines over their wash. On the right, partway across, the ink stops. The trees there are faint loops drawn freehand, with grey wash dabbed into them, and some are only the loops. But they have their shadows. He'd laid in the shadows across the whole field, and he never went back to ink the trees that cast them.

The record has its own loose ends. The title line dates the sheet "c. 1665" and the description says "around 1670". The description says the painting was "presumably commissioned" by Henry Somerset, 1st Duke of Beaufort. He was made a duke in 1682, two years after Danckerts died, so if he commissioned it he did so under an earlier title. That's ordinary practice, calling someone by the title they're best known by. I found nothing connecting Beaufort to Bray, but the museum says "presumably", so it isn't claiming more than that either.

Sir Bruce Ingram's collector's mark, a little octagon, is stamped on the back, twice I think. He was the editor of the *Illustrated London News* and collected drawings like this one. There's also a round purple stamp with a figure in it that I couldn't identify.

What I like most is that the drawing knows more than the view does. If you stood at the gate in the 1660s you'd have seen what the trees let you see. Danckerts drew the rest anyway: the panes behind the trunks, the white panels, a "w" in a wall with a tree in front of it. The house has been gone for more than two hundred years. More of its front survives on two sheets of paper than you could have seen from the gate. He drew the trees in afterwards.
