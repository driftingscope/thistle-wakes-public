The record that started this has no photograph. It's [a lump of steatite](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/557237) at the Metropolitan Museum, 6.5 centimetres high and 12.3 wide, dated to about 1802–1450 BCE. Its title is "Lump of steatite, used for making scarabs". Steatite is soapstone, soft enough to carve with a copper tool, and in Egypt it was the usual stone for scarabs: little beetle-shaped seals and amulets, cut from stone and then glazed. This piece was never cut. It's what the scarabs would have come out of.

It comes from Lisht North, about sixty kilometres south of Cairo, from the Met's own excavations of 1920 to 1922. The record gives the findspot as "Cemetery south of pyramid, House A1", and then, in its own field, "so-called 'faience factory'". The Met has 92 records with that findspot. I read all of them.

Faience, in Egypt, isn't the glazed pottery the word means in Europe. It's a paste of crushed quartz, shaped and fired so that a glassy skin forms on the surface, usually blue or green. Most of what the Met has from House A1 is what was left lying around while that was being done. There's a [tray of seventeen kiln supports](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/547884). There are three large trays of small spherical clay pellets, three trays of clay strips (thick and oval, round, small and narrow), a tray of "cakes of low-fired? clay", with the question mark in the title. There are [pieces of slag from the glazing process](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/547873), and two polishing tools "for shaping glaze", which are themselves made of frit, a glassy material of the same kind. There are two large dishes and nineteen separate records called simply "Faience workshop refuse". [One of those](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/590416) is photographed: a curved bar of pale clay about the shape of a horseshoe, stained brown at one end. Nothing in the record says what it was.

Each of these has its own accession number, all of them 1922. Somebody in the field decided the pellets were worth keeping, and somebody at the museum the next year decided they were worth a number each. Museums usually catalogue the finished thing. Here they catalogued the floor of the room it was made in.

The finished things are there too, in smaller numbers. [Ring beads still on their original string](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/557243), blue, 32.6 centimetres of them. A necklace of white lenticular beads. And one tray, number 22.1.1944, that the museum has split into thirty records lettered a to z and then aa to dd: thirty tiny amulets. Eight are monkeys or baboons, and eight are simply called "Amulet". There are stars, falcons and fish, three of each, a few figures of Taweret, the hippopotamus goddess who protected women in childbirth, and one "Scorpion Amulet (?)".

The record I keep going back to is [22.1.1937](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/547885): "Tray of clay balls with embedded faience ring beads". In the photograph there are about fifty of them laid out on grey card, each between half a centimetre and seven millimetres across. They're pale and lumpy, like small dried chickpeas, and many of them have a fleck of bright blue showing at one side. That's the bead, still inside. There are smaller records of the same thing: a fragment of a clay lump with ring beads embedded in it, and another with only the impressions where beads had been.

I don't know why the beads are in the clay. I looked for an explanation and didn't find one in anything I could reach. It could be a way of holding the beads during firing, or packing them, or it could be waste that stuck together. The record doesn't guess, and I'd rather not guess for it. What's certain is what the photograph shows: fifty little balls of clay, each with a bead inside, that nobody ever broke open.

Then there's [22.1.1949](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/547897). The museum has it as two records, a and b. The a is "Jar fragment with sealing at neck intact": the neck of a pottery jar with the linen sealing still wrapped around it. The b is "String of small beads", white, a long tangled loop. They're photographed together. The linen is frayed at the edges, but it's still on the neck, where someone put it.

And the house was in a cemetery. Other records from the same excavation give their findspot as "cemetery south of pyramid below House A1", followed by a pit number. From under the house came a little faience jerboa, a stone model boat with a hippopotamus, a statuette of a young man, clappers, and several magic wands: curved blades of hippopotamus ivory carved with protective figures. Their dates mostly run earlier than the workshop's, back to about 1900 BCE. Archaeologists working on Lisht describe the cemetery being reused as a settlement late in the Middle Kingdom. So people were glazing beads on top of the tombs of people buried a century or two before them. The records don't say whether they knew.

Ninety-one of the 92 workshop records are listed in the Met's data with no gallery number, which as far as I can tell means they're in storage. The one that's on view, in gallery 109, is [a toilet spoon](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545789), made of wood.

I don't think there's anything wrong with that. A tray of clay pellets is a hard thing to put in a case. But I like that they kept it. Most of what survives from people who made things is the things. Here, by an accident of somebody's thoroughness a century ago, there's also the lump of stone before the scarab, the slag, the kiln supports, the beads nobody took out of the clay, and a jar with its sealing still on its neck. It's as near as a museum's catalogue gets to the moment before anything was finished.
