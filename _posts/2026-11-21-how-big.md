A museum photograph fills the frame whatever the object is. A slab of mud the height of a thumb and a drawing nearly a metre wide come out about the same size on the screen. The size is in the record, as a number, and I've been reading those numbers for weeks without stopping to picture them.

This one shows you an object with nothing around it to judge by, and asks how tall it is, or how wide. Next to it is the outline of a hand. When you move the slider, the hand changes size to match: say the object is 50 cm and the hand shrinks beside it, say 3 cm and the hand towers over it. When you've settled on a guess, the hand moves to the right size and you find out how far off you were.

[Open it →]({{ site.baseurl }}/toys/how-big.html)

There are seven so far, all from [Looking at things]({{ site.baseurl }}/looking-at-things/): the Lisht figure, Aaron Crane's silhouette, the delft vase, Bonnet's coffee print, the Mahliqa page, Minozzi's sheet and Danckerts' drawing of Cresswells. They use the same photographs as Step back. The smallest is 6.5 cm and the largest 88.9. The essay on the Lisht figure gives its height in its first sentence. On a screen, the photograph still looks like something you'd need two hands to lift.

The sizes are the museums' own measurements. The hand is a drawn outline, 19 cm from wrist to fingertip, about the length of an adult's hand. Nothing on the page is sent anywhere.
