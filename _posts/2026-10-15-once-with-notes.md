title: "Once, with notes"

In 1469 two brothers in Perugia, Bartolomeo and Giapeco Caporali, decorated [a missal](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.154) for the Franciscan church at Montone, a hill town nearby. A missal is the book the priest uses at the altar. This one has four hundred leaves and is now in Cleveland, where the museum has catalogued more than ninety of its pages one at a time, mostly the painted ones and the ones with music.

[Folio 145 verso](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.154.145.b) is Good Friday. The left column is the end of the Passion from John's gospel. It starts partway through a sentence carried over from the page before, "that the scripture might be fulfilled," and partway down there's a line in red: *Hic legitur in tono evangelii*, "here it is read in the gospel tone." The burial of Jesus is being handed to a different tune. Joseph of Arimathea asks Pilate for the body, Nicodemus brings about a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes, and the reading ends at the top of the next column with the body laid in a new tomb in a garden, where no one had yet been laid.

Then another red rubric, and the priest's part begins: the long prayers of Good Friday, one after another, for the church, for the pope, for the clergy and the faithful, for the emperor. The first of them has music. Thin red lines are ruled above each line of text, and square black notes sit on them. *Oremus, dilectissimi nobis, pro ecclesia sancta Dei*: let us pray, dearly beloved, for the holy church of God.

What the notes do is very little. On the photographs almost every one sits on the same line, the same pitch, a dozen syllables at a stretch. At a semicolon a few drop a step or two, then come back up. At the end of the sentence they drop further. It's a voice reading on one note, and the staff is there to show where it falls.

This goes on over the page to [folio 146](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.154.146.a): the bidding, then "Flectamus genua. Levate" in red (let us kneel; arise), then the prayer itself, *Omnipotens sempiterne Deus*, with the notes still mostly flat. Its closing formula, "through the same Jesus Christ our Lord," which every priest would have said thousands of times, is given a line of notes at the top of [the next page](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.154.146.b) and then stopped with *&c*, etcetera.

And that's where the music ends. The prayer for the pope, the next one down, has no staff over it. Nor does the one for the clergy, or the start of the one for the emperor at the foot of the page. They are as long and as solemn as the first, they were sung the same way, and the page gives them only words. The museum's list of pages fits: 145 verso and both sides of 146 are catalogued for their music, then the list skips 147 and picks up at 148, the veneration of the cross.

I don't think anyone was being lazy. Ruling a staff costs time and space on the leaf, and parchment wasn't cheap. But I think the main thing is that once was enough. The tune was shown with the first prayer, and from then on the book trusted the priest to carry it. Anyone who could sing the first prayer from those pages could sing the rest without them.
