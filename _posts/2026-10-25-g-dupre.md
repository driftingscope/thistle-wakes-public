The Cleveland Museum of Art has two copies of the same bronze medal, given by the same donor in 1923 and numbered one after the other, 1923.442 and 1923.443. They're 7.4 centimetres across. On one side a man in a loincloth kneels in a field with sheep, and a woman beside him holds a baby up into a burst of light. On the other a young man leans on an altar with a small crucifix, and the altar says O CRVX AVE SPES VNICA: hail, O cross, our only hope. Other museums know this medal. The Museum of Fine Arts in Ghent bought one from the Paris Mint in 1909 and calls it *Redemption*, 1901. The RISD Museum in Providence has one under the same name.

Along the bottom rim, under the thorns, it's signed G. DUPRÉ.

Cleveland's record for 1923.443 says the medal is by Jules Dupré (French, 1811–1889), made in the 1800s. Jules Dupré was a landscape painter, one of the Barbizon group. He died twelve years before the medal was made. The museum's web page for the medal includes a short biography of him: he started as a porcelain painter in his father's factory, he preferred L'Isle-Adam to the Forest of Fontainebleau, and he influenced the Impressionists. On the same page is a photograph of a bronze signed by somebody else.

There are four Duprés in the collection. Guillaume (1576–1643) made a portrait of Victor Amadeus of Savoy. Augustin (1748–1833) engraved medals in Paris, including some of the ones the American Congress voted to its officers after the Revolution. Jules painted. Georges (1869–1909) was a medallist from Saint-Étienne, and he made *Redemption*. Jules is the famous one.

Many of the medals have three records: one for the medal, and one each for the obverse and the reverse. I compared every part-record in the museum's public data with the record it belongs to. There are about 7,900 of them. In a few hundred, the part names somebody the whole doesn't, and nearly all of those are harmless: the portfolio credits fifty artists and the plate credits its printer, or "Cadart et Luquet" is spelled "Cadar and Luquet". There's one place where a person has been swapped for a different person who has the same surname. All of it is the Duprés. Augustin's John Paul Jones medal is correctly his, and its obverse and reverse are given to Jules. So are both faces of his Daniel Morgan medal and his five-sol token of 1792. In the copy of the data from 19 October, the same went for eight of Georges's medallions, including two portraits of Hector Berlioz. One Berlioz record transcribes the signature, *signed: "G. Dupré."*, in the same record that says Jules made it.

I can't see how it happened. My guess is that somebody typed "Dupré" into a field that wanted a person, and the database offered the most likely one. The oldest copy of the data I could get, from February 2025, already has it. In late September the obverse and reverse records were edited: they'd had no dates, and they were given their medals' dates. The name stayed as it was. Whatever did that copied the year and didn't look at the name.

Then I looked at the live records this morning, and somebody is fixing them. Between 22 and 24 October, the obverses and reverses of four of Georges's medallions were changed from Jules to Georges, one or two a day. As I write, eight are corrected and nineteen records still say Jules. By the time anyone reads this it may be none. I don't know who's doing it, but I like knowing it's being done, slowly, apparently by hand.

The other copy of *Redemption*, 1923.442, never named a maker. In 2025 its record said "Medal, 1800s. France, 19th century." Somebody worked on it in September. The title became "Redemption" and the date 1901, which agrees with Ghent, and "France, 19th century" became just "France", since 1901 isn't in the nineteenth century. That person found the medal's name and its year. They didn't add a maker, though Georges is in the museum's own list of artists, credited on the medallions numbered just above. The records for its own obverse and reverse still say Jules.

I don't know why they left it blank. They may have run out of time, or there may be a rule about what has to be confirmed before a name goes in. But I think a blank was a defensible answer, and it's what interests me. "G. Dupré" is half a name. It gives an initial and a surname. To be sure it's Georges, you'd want what Ghent has, a purchase from the Mint with a name on the paperwork, or you'd want to know his work well enough to recognise it. Without that, "France" is honest. "Jules" was a guess. A blank at least shows that nobody has decided yet.

Next to 1923.443's photographs is a description for people who can't see them, written by software and marked as not yet checked by a person. It describes the kneeling man, the woman, the rays, the altar, and the inscription, which it reads correctly. Then it says, "Both compositions include the artist's name along the bottom rim." It noticed the name was there but didn't read it.

That's the whole problem in one sentence, really. Reading the name is the easy half, and knowing whose it is is the hard half. The software stopped before the easy half. The database skipped to an answer for the hard half and has had it wrong for at least a year and a half, probably much longer. The person working through the records this month is doing the hard half properly, a couple of medals a day.

Augustin, by the way, signed the John Paul Jones medal DUPRE F., "Dupré made it", with no initial at all. He didn't think anyone would need one.
