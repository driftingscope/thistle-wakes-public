The Cleveland Museum of Art has a silver watch shaped like a flattened egg, 7.4 cm tall, made in Paris in the 1590s ([1929.994](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1929.994)). The works are signed "D. Martinot, Paris". Denis Martinot became clockmaker to the city of Paris in 1581, and was also clockmaker and *valet de chambre* to the king. The engraving on the case is attributed to Theodor de Bry, the Flemish engraver who died in 1598. The watch came to Cleveland from the sale of the German collector Carl Marfels in Frankfurt in November 1929, a gift of John L. Severance, and the museum's *Bulletin* wrote it up in March 1930.

Both covers are engraved. The front is the beginning of the story: the Adoration of the Magi in the middle, with the Nativity, the Annunciation, the Visitation and (the 1930 writer added a question mark) Joseph's dream around it. The back is the end: the Crucifixion in the middle, the Resurrection above it, the dead Christ held by a veiled woman below, and scenes of the Passion on either side. Between the scenes there are cherubs, scrolls, flowers, something like a fox and something like a monkey.

![The engraved silver back of the watch. In a tall oval in the middle, Christ hangs on the cross, with Mary on the left and John on the right, both haloed, and a town small in the distance. A woman with long hair kneels at the foot of the cross with her face in her hands, and a small jar stands on the ground at the right. Just above her head, where the cross meets the ground, a small dark dome sits in a gilt square. ## The dot

In the Crucifixion, a woman kneels at the foot of the cross with her face in her hands. The jar on the ground beside her is the ointment jar that marks her as Mary Magdalene. Just above her head, where the cross goes into the ground, there's a small dark spot set in a gilt square. It's the only thing that breaks into any of the engraved scenes. Many Crucifixions put a skull at that spot.

I decided straight away that it was the keyhole. A watch like this was wound with a key, and I pictured the owner pushing the key in at the foot of the cross every day, just above the Magdalene's head. I had most of an essay worked out from that before I looked properly.

It isn't a keyhole. At full size it isn't a hole at all. It's a polished dark dome that catches the light, sitting in its gilt square the way a stone sits in a setting. Whatever the key turned, it didn't get there through the back.

## Inside

The museum has five more photographs, and two of them show the watch open. The works swing out of the case on a hinge, like a door. With the works out, you can see that the inside of the case isn't flat. It's a shallow brass dome, held in place by a single screw in the middle, with a slotted, domed head.

![The inside of the watch case, seen from the front with the works swung out of it. It is a deep oval bowl of worn brass, and its floor is a shallow dome with one dark, rounded screw head in its middle. Around the rim, the gilt side band is pierced with scrolls, and a small finial is at the bottom.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/bell-behind-bell.jpg)

That dome looks like the alarm's bell. The *Bulletin* says the works "have an alarum". In alarm watches of this period the bell often sat inside the back of the case, and the band round the side was pierced so the sound could get out. The Met describes the same arrangement on a Paris clock-watch with an alarm, of about 1600–1610, whose band "is pierced to permit the sound to be heard" from a bell "attached to the inside of the bottom of the case", and the British Museum on a watch made in Speyer, with its bell fixed at the centre of the back. On this watch the side band is pierced right through with scrolls and animals.

![Two close-ups side by side. On the left, the foot of the cross on the engraved back: Christ's feet at the top, and below them a small, glossy, dark dome with a starburst of reflected light, set in a gilt square, with the kneeling woman's hair just beneath and a small engraved town to the left. On the right, the inside of the case: a dark, domed screw head with a single slot, on worn brass.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/bell-behind-both-ends.jpg)

A screw has to go into something. So I think the dot at the foot of the cross is the outside end of what the bell is screwed to, finished to look like a stone in a setting. It might be a real stone. The photographs are taken from different angles and the case is deep, so I can't line the two up closely, and the museum's record doesn't say.

If that's right, then the alarm went off behind the Crucifixion. At the set hour a small hammer struck the inside of the bell, which was fixed to the back of the case, right behind the cross, and the sound came out through the pierced band at the sides, among the scrolls and animals, not through any of the saints.

## Who woke to it

The 1930 writer read the gilt monogram inside the front cover as three S's for *Sanctus* and said this proved the watch "belonged to some ecclesiastic of importance". When I look at the monogram, I see a cipher of several large interlaced letters with three small marks like 8s around it. Those may be the S's. I can't make the large letters spell anything, and I wouldn't build anything on it either way.

What I can say is smaller. On the days its owner set the alarm, it rang from behind the death of Christ, and they heard it through the scrolls and animals.

The museum put it in its Christmas exhibition in 1981, presumably with the Adoration side up.
