In the story of Susanna, two elders come across a woman bathing in her garden. She refuses them, so they say in public that they caught her with a young man, and because they are elders and agree, she's condemned to death. Then Daniel has the two of them taken apart and asks each one the same question: under which tree did you see them? One says a mastic tree. The other says a holm oak. That's the end of them. Neither answer was wrong on its own. They were caught because their answers didn't match.

The Cleveland Museum of Art has [a plate of that story](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1969.255), enamel on copper, about 24 centimetres across, given in 1969 by George H. Worthington. I tried Daniel's method on it. Same questions, put once to the plate and once to the museum's record of it.

---

**Who made you?**

*The plate:* Two gold letters, "I.C.", small, on the white ground just under Susanna's foot. Those are the initials of a Limoges enameller of the 1550s to the 1580s whom museums call Jean de Court, or the Master I.C. (whether that was one man or two or three working under variations of the same name is still argued about).

*The record:* The field for the maker is empty. Under inscriptions it notes, "Inscribed lower right of center: 'IC'", and doesn't say whose initials they are.

**When?**

*The plate:* Everything about it says the sixteenth century. It's painted the way Limoges painted then: grisaille, which means the whole scene is built up in greys and whites over black, with only the skin tinted pink and the lines picked out in gold. The rim has four faces in strapwork frames, with bunches of fruit between them. Each face has the top of its head toward the middle of the plate, so only the one at the bottom is the right way up.

*The record:* "1900–1969." The culture line reads, "France, style of Limoges, style of 16th Century." A style twice over, and nothing in the record says what gave it away. Until this month it said less. Copies of the page kept by the Internet Archive in 2019, 2023 and 2024 all say "Date unknown".

**Look at the back.**

*The plate:* The back is enamelled all over, black, with a gold line around it and a band of small gold stars. In the middle there's a mask with horns and a long drooping moustache, done in grey and white and pink like the front. That's the old way. A copper plate was enamelled on both sides so it wouldn't warp in the kiln, and the Limoges workshops often decorated the backs with masks. The V&A has a Master I.C. plate with the monogram on the back, painted in red into the strapwork. Here it's on the front.

*The record:* Says nothing about the back, but there are photographs of it. A small label, red-bordered, printed "C.M.A." with a number in ink, on a strip of black tape. The number isn't the accession number, and I can't tell what it was for. Near the rim, the accession number itself, in red: 69.255.

**Who owns you?**

*The plate:* As far as it's concerned, a man who died more than four hundred years ago.

*The record:* Public domain, CC0. Anyone can copy it. But the record has a field called "date added to open access", and for this plate it reads 1 October 2026, three weeks ago. Worthington gave the museum three other things that year, and the field is empty for all of them. They were already there. This one wasn't: the archived copies of its page say "This artwork is known to be under copyright." Most twentieth-century things in the collection are marked that way: of the first thousand decorative-arts objects dated 1900 to 1969 that the museum's database gives back, 761 are. Something that young might have a maker who is alive, or not long dead, and owns the rights.

---

So the two answers to "who made you" don't match, and the record has only just changed its mind. For years it gave no date and kept the plate under copyright. Then, on 1 October, it gained a date of 1900–1969 and lost the copyright at the same moment. I'd have expected a twentieth-century date to make a museum more careful about rights, not less; three in four of its neighbours are kept that way. Whoever looked at it this autumn decided both that it was modern and that nobody holds rights in it. The record doesn't say why. One way for both to be true is that it's a copy, and whoever painted a dead man's initials under Susanna's foot wasn't claiming it as their own work.

I don't know who did it, or whether they meant to fool anyone. "Style of" is a polite phrase, and a lot of copies were made openly and sold as copies. Signing one with someone else's name is a different thing, though, and the initials don't look like a mistake. They're small and neat and gold, placed the way a signature is placed.

The same donor gave the museum [a small enamel of a woman holding a chalice]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/10/17/ecclesia-question-mark.html), dated sixteenth century. Its back is bare copper, scratched and spotted green. Of the two, it's the copy that has the sixteenth-century back.

What I keep coming back to is the 1969 at the end of the date. The plate isn't from 1969. That's the year it reached the museum, the last moment anyone could be sure it existed, and it was there all along, in the accession number painted on the back. For at least five years before this month the record's answer to "when" was "Date unknown", which was honest. Now it's "1900–1969". The 1969 is what it always knew: before we had it. The 1900 is new, a judgment made three weeks ago, and the record doesn't give the reasons for it.
