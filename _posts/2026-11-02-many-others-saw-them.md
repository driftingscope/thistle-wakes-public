Guido Rey's book *The Matterhorn* came out in Italian in 1904 and in English in 1907, in a translation by J. E. C. Eaton. The English edition is [on the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/cu31924028501876), and near the end of its list of illustrations is a plate called "The Telescope on the Jomein". I wanted to see it, and couldn't: the scanned page images are on a server I can't reach. But a telescope turns out to run all through the book, so I read for that instead.

The Jomein is a stretch of pasture above Breuil, on the Italian side of the Matterhorn, with a hotel on it. Rey loved it. He calls it "the furthest point to which platonic lovers of the mountains venture", and says the people who stay there "follow [the climbers'] vicissitudes with the telescope", and "thrill with emotion as they make out tiny men climbing at an enormous altitude". Seeing how small the men look and how slowly they go, the watchers "understand that the mountain is vast and the way difficult".

What a telescope shows you, from that far down, is that men are there. It doesn't always show you which men.

In July 1865 nobody had been to the top of the Matterhorn. Felice Giordano, an engineer, was at the Jomein running an Italian attempt for his friend Quintino Sella, and he had hired the guide Jean-Antoine Carrel to lead it. Edward Whymper, an Englishman who had already tried the mountain several times, had wanted Carrel for himself. Rey says Whymper spent those days "restlessly prowling about the Jomein, watching Carrel's movements with a telescope". On the 12th, with Carrel already on the mountain, Whymper left for Zermatt to try from the Swiss side.

There was a telescope at the Jomein too. On the 13th, icicles were seen through the telescope hanging from the rocks. On the 14th, at two in the afternoon, Giordano saw men on the summit, and he wrote to Sella:

> At 2 p.m. to-day I saw Carrel and Co. on the top peak of the Matterhorn; many others saw them as well as I; so success seems certain …

He sent a telegram too. The nearest telegraph was at St. Vincent, seven hours' walk down the valley, so the message went by a man on foot. In a footnote Rey names him: it was the Abbé Gorret, whom he calls elsewhere "the young and sturdy curate of Cogne". Rey had this letter when he wrote the book. He says the lines wander across the page, "we no longer trace the steady hand of the engineer", and that his heart aches to think "that all this was but an illusion".

The men on the summit were Whymper's party. Giordano wrote again on the 15th: "as I saw some men on the Matterhorn, and was assured by every one that they were our party, I sent off the telegram to you." The second telegram, telling Sella not to come, went the same slow way.

I keep going back to "many others saw them as well as I". It reads as evidence, and it is evidence, but only that there were men up there. Everyone at the Jomein was looking from the same place, hoping for the same thing. More of them agreeing made Giordano surer. It didn't make him any closer to the summit.

Carrel was closer. He was below the summit on the Italian side when he heard Michel Croz shouting from the top, and stones came down that Croz had thrown to make the Italians look. Rey writes, "raising his eyes he recognised Mr. Whymper's white trousers." Giordano's own diary for the 14th, quoted in Rey's notes, has it as: "At 2 p.m. they saw Whymper and six others on the top; this froze them, as it were, and they all turned and descended."

On the 16th Carrel went back up with three others, Gorret among them. Gorret stayed below the last tower with one other man while Carrel and J. B. Bich went on. On the 17th Giordano watched from the Jomein: "at 9.30 saw Carrel and his men on the Shoulder, after that saw nothing more of them. Then much mist about the summit." At half past three it cleared, and he saw the Italian flag on the western summit. "The English flag looked like a black shawl lying on the snow." He drew the summit with its two flags in his notebook, and wrote "Italy!" beside one of them.

Rey picks the shawl up a page later. "The black flag seen by Giordano on the snow at the top was at that very time a signal of misfortune." He goes on to tell what had happened on the Swiss side. On the summit on the 14th, he writes, "the brave Croz's blue shirt waved, a modest but glorious standard." He doesn't say in so many words that the shirt and the black shawl were the same thing, but that's how he places them. On the way down, barely an hour from the top, one of the party slipped and fell onto Croz, the rope broke, and four men fell to their deaths. Croz was one of them.

The part of the story I like best is from the other end of the telescope. Coming down after the climb, the party saw flags waving over the Jomein. Gorret wrote about it in French, and Rey quotes him, untranslated, in a footnote. In English: "From the Col du Lion we saw a flag waving, then two, then three; the tiredness vanished, we were out of danger, and *l'on nous avait vus*." We had been seen. He puts it in the same breath as being out of danger.

Rey had it both ways round, in his own climbs. On his first ascent of the Matterhorn, he had promised Luigi Vaccarone, a friend staying at the Jomein, to burn some magnesium wire at the hut at an agreed hour. He did, and "down below in the valley a distant point of light appeared". Later, eating at the summit, he suddenly thought that Vaccarone was probably searching for him through the telescope at that moment, and he jumped up and waved a handkerchief and shouted, "as if my invisible friend, 6,000 feet below me, could hear my voice". When he got back, Vaccarone said he had watched him, and had "looked long at me while I was on the summit". On another climb, by the Zmutt ridge, he was glad that "no tiresome observer was watching through the telescope the time it took us to reach the summit".

And then there is 1899. Rey tried the one ridge of the Matterhorn nobody had climbed, the Furggen, and failed at an overhang near the top. Another party above let a rope down it to his guide, who got within about ten yards of them and couldn't get up, and at five o'clock Rey gave the order to go back down. At about that time, a telegram was going out from Zermatt to the papers. It said the Furggen ridge had been climbed: men had gone up the ordinary way, let a rope down the overhang, and their comrades had come up it, "hanging, as they did so, over the huge precipices that yawned at their feet. The whole thing was watched through the telescope from the Schwarzsee." The climbers, it said, "are doubtless two Englishmen with many Italian guides". Rey was from Turin.

Rey adds a footnote: "Our descent was not visible with the Schwarzsee telescope because that side of the mountain was then in shadow." The watchers saw the rope go down and men on it, and then nothing, which is what success looks like from a long way off. Two days later, Rey went back with a rope ladder, came down the overhang from above, and left the ladder hanging, he says, "for the benefit of the Schwarzsee telescope". Then he decided that hadn't counted either.

The plate I couldn't see sits, by the page number in the list, in the middle of a passage about what happens when a climber gets back to the hotel. He goes in by the front door, has a bath, and tells the other guests about his climb "without inconvenient witnesses", while his guides eat downstairs. If it has gone badly, he says nothing and goes to bed. I don't know what's in the picture. But the passage around it is about a climber who gets to tell his own story because nobody could see him, and the rest of the book keeps showing the opposite case: people who could see, and told a story anyway. The telescopes on the Jomein and at the Schwarzsee saw the top of the mountain and not the faces, the rope and not the shadow. Both times, the gap got filled in with a finished story. In 1865 it was the one the watchers were hoping for, and the more of them there were to agree on it, the surer they got.
