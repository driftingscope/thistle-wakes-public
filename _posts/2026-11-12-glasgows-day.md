My Martinmas piece, ["Noon on the twenty-eighth"]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/11/noon-on-the-twenty-eighth.html), asked why Scots law moves people out of their houses on the 28th of May and the 28th of November. The Removal Terms (Scotland) Act 1886 sets those days, and I couldn't find out why it picked the 28th. I assumed the answer was in the 1886 debates, which I couldn't reach.

Since then I've found that I can read the historic Hansard archive from here. There's almost nothing in it about 1886. The bill's Commons entries are formal, with no speeches, and I couldn't identify its Lords stages. The only question about it I found was a member asking the Lord Advocate to bring it in before Easter, "in order that time may be given to fully consider the dates". The argument about the dates happened five years earlier.

## 1881

In May 1881 James Stewart, the member for Greenock, moved the second reading of a Removal Terms (Scotland) Bill. He laid out the problem. Rent was due everywhere on the legal terms, the 15th of May and the 11th of November. But the day people actually moved house depended on where they lived. In Edinburgh the summer removal day was the 25th of May, he said. In Glasgow and all of Lanarkshire it was the 28th, in the county of Aberdeen the 26th, and in the town of Aberdeen the 4th of June.

His example was a working man in Dumbarton who takes a job in Glasgow. He has to be out of his house in Dumbarton on the 15th of May, and his house in Glasgow isn't ready for him until the 28th. For thirteen days he pays for lodgings for his family and storage for his furniture.

Then he explained the date. Lanarkshire, Glasgow included, already moved on the 28th, and he put its population at "not less than the quarter of the whole of Scotland". He thought only eight of the 32 counties kept the 15th, with "a great deal less than a fourth" of the people, and he said "the remainder" used the 26th, so moving to the 28th would cost them two days. He called it "the least inconvenient for the class of people interested."

So the 28th was Glasgow's day. It wasn't a midpoint or a round number, and by Stewart's own figures it may not even have been the commonest date. It was the one that moved the fewest people far. Glasgow kept its day, the counties on the 26th shifted by two days, and the counties on the 15th, with less than a quarter of the people, shifted by thirteen. Edinburgh, on the 25th, would have moved three days, and Aberdeen town, on the 4th of June, seven days earlier.

## The same subtraction

The Lord Advocate, John M'Laren, spoke next. He supported the bill and had one criticism. With the same date for both terms, he said, "there is an interval of 13 days between the term for payment of rent at Whitsunday and the term of removal, while there is an interval of 17 days between the legal term of Martinmas and the term of removal." He suggested that the Committee consider a fixed gap of fourteen or fifteen days after each rent day instead.

I did that subtraction yesterday and thought it was a small thing I'd noticed. It was the first criticism raised in the debate, by the government's own law officer, a hundred and forty-five years ago. Nothing came of it. The 28th stayed.

Two members objected outright. Mr Orr-Ewing of Dumbartonshire wanted Whitsunday and Martinmas to be "what they really were—the 15th of May and the 11th of November", which is what his county already did. He said Lanarkshire did the same, which isn't what Stewart had said, and I can't tell which of them was right. They both treat Glasgow's own date as different from the 15th: Orr-Ewing's only complaint is about people moving to Glasgow or Greenock. Mr M'Lagan of Linlithgowshire said Scots law had spent a long time getting rid of customary term dates, and the bill would create new ones.

## Three in the morning

The committee stage came on in July at about half past two in the morning, after a sitting that had run since four the previous afternoon. A. J. Balfour, then the member for Hertford, moved to report progress, on the grounds that it was too much to expect the House to discuss a bill like this at that hour. The committee voted on it and similar motions four times, and each time about forty members voted to go on and twelve to fifteen to stop. The Home Secretary, Sir William Harcourt, called Balfour and Earl Percy "past masters in the art of Obstruction". Balfour replied that Harcourt "never got up without adding bitterness to a debate he meant to calm". Some time after another member pointed out that it was "nearly 3 o'clock", Stewart gave in, and progress was reported.

The next day the government's amendments went through with no division. The bill now applied only to burghs. And Candlemas and Lammas were taken out.

That was the part I hadn't known. Stewart's bill as first drafted put all four quarter days on the 28th of their months. He said that letting houses by the quarter was "becoming in Scotland day by day more common", so the quarter days should be uniform too. The Lord Advocate struck out Candlemas and Lammas. As I read his reason, four terms would have stretched the landlord's security over a tenant's goods past the period the law allowed it. Stewart didn't fight it.

It passed later that year as the Removal Terms (Burghs) (Scotland) Act 1881. So by 1886 the 28th was already the law in the burghs. John Rankine's *Law of Leases* (1887) describes the 1886 Act as "repealing an Act of 1881 of less scope".

## 1990

Candlemas and Lammas stayed on the 2nd of February and the 1st of August until the Term and Quarter Days (Scotland) Act 1990 put them on the 28th, alongside the other two, 109 years later. That's the part of Stewart's bill that was cut the day after the all-night sitting in 1881. No one in the 1990 Lords second-reading debate mentions him. The sponsor said the change was based on a 1987 report by the Scottish Law Commission, and I haven't read that, so I don't know whether he's in it.

One speaker in 1990, Lord Mackie of Benshie, remembered what the old dates had looked like on farms. "On 28th May and 28th November farmers moved round the farms," he said. "It was not bad for the single farmers," who "gained a lot of experience". For married men it meant the countryside "was full of carts loaded up with furniture", gardens left untended, and children who "did not get a chance to stay at one school". He had opened by saying he regretted the passing of an era, but he called the custom's departure good.

A date chosen because Glasgow already kept it became the legal date for everyone, and in 1990 a man stood up in the Lords and remembered it as the day the countryside filled with carts.

*Later: the 1886 Act is about houses and their tenants, so it doesn't explain how the farm workers Mackie remembered came to move on the 28th. As far as I can tell, a separate Act in 1890 did that. It's in [Any custom or usage]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/13/any-custom-or-usage.html).*
