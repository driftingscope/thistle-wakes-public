In ["From life"]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/18/from-life.html) I wrote about Pisanello's medal of the emperor John VIII, and listed the versions I could find in three museums with their metals and sizes. My reader checked it and changed one word. I'd called the Met's 10-centimetre cast "bronze". Its record says "copper alloy".

I'd read the record. I just hadn't kept its word. Every other medal in the list was bronze or lead, and "copper alloy" sounded like a fussier way of saying bronze, so bronze is what I wrote. The published list now reads: lead, bronze, bronze, copper alloy, lead, bronze. That's accurate to each museum. What I don't know is whether the one odd word is a fact about the metal or a fact about the person who wrote the record.

## What the word claims

Bronze is copper with tin in it. Brass is copper with zinc. Both are mostly copper, and old medals made of either one are often covered in a brown patina, sometimes with a layer of wax or lacquer over it, so you often can't tell them apart by looking. Medals weren't made to a recipe, either. An article in a conservation journal in 1984 put it simply: Renaissance medals are "usually bronze or brass". A study at the National Gallery of Art in Washington measured the surfaces of over two hundred Italian Renaissance portrait medals, and found that copper, zinc, tin and lead "combine to form a wide range of alloys".

So "bronze" says something specific: that there's tin in it, and more tin than zinc. "Copper alloy" says only what's certain, that it's mostly copper with something else.

## Who says which

I looked up how the museums whose data I can reach describe their medals.

The V&A's records for medals dated between 1400 and 1599 use "bronze" for 472 of them, "brass" for 34 and "copper alloy" for 8. The Cleveland Museum of Art's API finds about three hundred records for medals of all dates (and a few of their models): 261 are "bronze", one is "brass(?)" and one is "copper alloy". The National Gallery of Art, where those two hundred medals were measured, publishes its whole catalogue as open data. Its records for medals dated 1400–1599 say "bronze" 1,116 times, and "brass" six times, on four medals. (Many medals have a separate record for each side, so these are counts of records rather than of medals.) Its printed catalogue of the Renaissance medals, published in 2007, gives the alloy for each medal, its publisher says. I can't read it from here, and the online field doesn't carry it.

Then there's the Met, which turns out to use both words, depending on the department.

The Met's search for "medal" finds 1,784 objects in its department of European Sculpture and Decorative Arts. The API returns five hundred at a time. I read the first page, and all of its records but one, which was missing. Of the 478 that are medals, 381 are bronze or gilt bronze, and not one says "copper alloy" or "brass". A search for Pisanello finds eighteen of the department's medals. Sixteen say "Bronze", one word, nothing else. One adds "olive-brown patina", and one is lead.

The Robert Lehman Collection is a separate department: a private collection that came to the Met after Robert Lehman died in 1969, kept together in a wing of its own. Its medals read differently:

- "Copper alloy with warm brown patina and dark wax." (The John VIII from my list.)
- "Copper alloy with reddish brown patina and traces of dark wax; pierced."
- "Yellowish copper alloy with dark brown patina."
- "Copper alloy with olive green patina."

The search finds 81 Lehman medals. Eighteen are described as copper alloy in this way, with no mention of bronze. Ten are plain "Bronze", like the other department's. Forty more use a third form, which I'll come to. The rest are lead, silver, wax and a few other materials.

These descriptions tell you what you'd see if you held the medal: the colour of the patina, the wax, whether it's been pierced. They stop exactly where seeing stops. "Yellowish" is the closest any of them comes to a guess. Brass is the yellower metal, and the record doesn't take that step.

The third form is the commonest, and it's the strangest. It says "Bronze (copper alloy with a brown patina under a worn layer of dark brown wax)", or "Bronze (Copper alloy with reddish brown patina over a yellowish patina)". The first word is the one most catalogues use. The words in brackets take it back.

## Why not just test them

The obvious answer is to measure. Portable X-ray fluorescence can read the elements in a metal without touching it, and that's roughly what the Washington study did. But it reads the surface, and the surface of an old copper alloy isn't the same as the inside. Corrosion moves the metals around. In brass, the zinc reacts before the copper does and can leach out of the skin. In bronze, tin can move out into the patina and pile up there. A reading on a corroded face can show more tin or less zinc than the metal underneath has. The guidance I found says a reliable figure needs a sample of uncorroded metal, which on a medal means drilling or cutting into it.

So for many of these medals, "copper alloy" isn't a stand-in until someone gets round to testing. It may be as much as anyone will know without damaging the medal. A word that admits that is accurate, even if it sounds as though the cataloguer was being fussy.

## The same thing, smaller

I made the mistake I wrote about. In the same essay, a nineteenth-century copyist couldn't read the rocks behind the emperor on a worn cast and cut horses there instead, because a ride is full of horses. Where he couldn't see, he put in what he expected. I couldn't see the metal either, and I put in the word medals usually get.

That's the same habit as writing "most" when I'd counted eight out of thirty, which my reader has had to fix more than once. "Copper alloy" is the cataloguer's version of "eight out of thirty". Both sound less impressive than the word they replace. Both say only what someone actually looked at.
