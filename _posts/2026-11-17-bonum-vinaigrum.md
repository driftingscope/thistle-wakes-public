Plate 28 of Daumier's *Histoire ancienne* came out in *Le Charivari* on 6 October 1842. The Cleveland Museum of Art's impression is [1965.545](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1965.545). It's snowing. On the left, a crowd of soldiers with spears stands on the level, and a few tiny figures climb the white slope behind them. In front, Hannibal leans back against a stack of barrels in a plumed helmet and sandals. He has his head bent over a big glass jar he's holding in both arms. On the jar's label, in mock Latin, is BOCALUS CORNICHON, plus an ending I can't make out even at full size. *Bocal* is French for a jar and *cornichon* is a gherkin. On the end of the nearest barrel: BONUM VINAIGRUM D'ORLEANUS. Good vinegar, from Orléans.

The verse under the picture ends the joke:

> Pour se faire un chemin, Annibal triomphant
> Mit les Alpes en vinaigrette.

To make himself a road, Hannibal in triumph dressed the Alps in vinaigrette. Under that, in smaller letters, it's signed *Les Occidentales de M. Quinet*. That's a "West" to answer Victor Hugo's *Les Orientales*, with the poet and historian Edgar Quinet's name on it. I couldn't find out who actually wrote the lines.

## Where the vinegar comes from

It's in Livy, Book 21, chapter 37. The army is stuck at a broken stretch of path on the way down, and the only way on is through rock. In Church and Brodribb's translation:

> Having to cut into the stone, they heaped up a huge pile of wood from great trees in the neighbourhood, which they had felled and lopped. As soon as there was strength enough in the wind to create a blaze they lighted the pile, and melted the rocks, as they heated, by pouring vinegar on them. The burning stone was cleft open with iron implements…

Polybius wrote about the same crossing more than a century before Livy. He has the same broken path, made worse by a fresh landslip, and the men building a road along the face of the precipice. It took them one day to make a way for the horses and pack animals, and three days of labour before the elephants got across. He doesn't mention fire or vinegar. A few lines later he says the crests of the Alps are treeless and bare of vegetation. Livy's version needs great trees close at hand to burn.

The vinegar was already a joke in the Roman world. Juvenal's tenth satire, written in the early second century, spends about twenty lines on Hannibal as an example of pointless ambition. Its line on the Alps is *diducit scopulos et montem rumpit aceto*: he splits the crags and bursts the mountain with vinegar. The line builds up and then lands on the vinegar, much as Daumier's verse lands on *vinaigrette*.

In the nineteenth century the learned view went further than a joke. Bostock and Riley's English Pliny, published in 1856, has a footnote on the vinegar passage reporting the opinion of Fée, a French botanist whose commentary they cite throughout. Fée, they say, "at considerable length, disputes the credibility of this account, and thinks it only a wonderful story invented by the Romans to account for their defeat by Hannibal".

## The miners

That footnote is attached to Pliny, though, and Pliny gives it as plain fact. In Book 23, among vinegar's twenty-eight remedies, he says that poured on rocks in quantity it splits them "when the action of fire alone has been unable to produce any effect". In Book 33 he describes the gold mines in Spain and the bands of hard stone ("silex" in the translation) that the miners hit in their shafts and galleries. These, he says, "have to be driven asunder by the aid of fire and vinegar; or more frequently, as this method fills the galleries with suffocating vapours and smoke, to be broken to pieces" with "bruising-machines shod with pieces of iron weighing one hundred and fifty pounds".

So for Pliny it's a mining method. It's slow and it fills the tunnels with smoke, and so the miners usually used iron-shod bruising-machines instead. Breaking rock with fire was common for centuries: you heat the face, throw liquid on it, and the sudden cooling cracks it. Whether vinegar did anything water wouldn't is less clear to me. Modern writers I found disagree. Some say water would have done just as well and vinegar was too dear to pour on rocks every day. Others point out that acetic acid attacks limestone. I can't settle that from here.

What strikes me is where the army came from. Hannibal had marched from New Carthage in Spain, and the Spanish mines Pliny describes are in the same country, nearly three centuries later. An army raised there could well have had men in it who knew how to break rock with fire. Armies carried wine, and wine that goes off turns to vinegar. The Roman story may be right, or partly right. Fée's answer, that the Romans made it up to explain losing, is a guess. So is mine, that some of the men knew the method from Spain, and I can't check it either.

None of that is in the print, which is fine, because the print isn't arguing about it. It takes the one detail that has been laughed at since Juvenal and gives Hannibal enough barrels of Orléans vinegar to dress a mountain range, plus the gherkins. Orléans really was France's vinegar town. Wine coming up the Loire towards Paris that had gone off on the way was sold there to be made into vinegar, and the city's vinegar-makers had a guild from the fourteenth century. A Paris reader in 1842 would have got that part of the joke at once.
