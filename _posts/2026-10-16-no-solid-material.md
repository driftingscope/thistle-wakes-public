In January 1931 the Guelph Treasure went on show in Cleveland, and in one month 97,000 people came to see it. The centrepiece was [a portable altar](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1931.462) made for Countess Gertrude of Brunswick around 1045: a gold box about 27 centimetres long and 10 high, set with stones and enamel, with a slab of white-speckled porphyry on top for the priest to say Mass on. Nobody in those lines saw what was inside it. There's a small trap door in the bottom, locked with a key, and the key didn't come with the altar. The museum didn't open it until the 1980s. There's a new key now; an art handler at the museum made one.

Every altar was supposed to hold relics, and this one does, in ten small bundles. Each has a scrap of parchment with a saint's name on it in a medieval hand. Some are still wrapped in their old silks. An inventory of 1482 lists the altar's relics, and many of the ones it names are still there.

More recently the museum X-rayed the bundles, with help from the Cleveland Museum of Natural History, and wrote down what the pictures showed. The results are on each bundle's page in the online collection, which catalogues all ten as separate objects. Saint Vincent is part of the bone at the back of the skull, where the spine meets it. Saint Januarius is a nearly complete ankle bone. Saint Gregory is the top of a shinbone, where it widens into the knee. Saint Bartholomew is mummified flesh, which fits the story that he was flayed. Saint Adelaide is probably a finger bone, though the curve is wrong for a human; nobody can be sure without unwrapping it, and it hasn't been unwrapped. Another bundle has ten names on its label (Stephen, Sophie, Perpetua, Paul, and six more) and the X-ray found specks.

Two of them have nothing in them that an X-ray can see. One is [Saint Marcian](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1931.462.h), a twist of green silk tied with two cords, one brown and one pink. The other is [Saint Gertrude](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1931.462.g), a knot of rose-coloured silk with a parchment tag tied on with thread. As far as I can read it, the tag says *Gertrudis*. I don't know which Gertrude, or whether the countess chose a saint with her own name on purpose. The record doesn't say.

What the record does say is in the field for what the object is made of. For Adelaide it's "bone, plain weave blue silk." For Gertrude it's "plain weave variant rose colored silk with an extant selvage." That's all of it. The selvage is the finished edge a loom leaves along the side of a length of cloth, so somebody cut this piece from the side of the cloth, and that edge is still on it.

There are two ordinary explanations, and the museum doesn't pick one. Bone that small can go to dust over enough centuries, and a pinch of dust may not show on an X-ray at all. Or there was never a bone. A cloth that had touched a saint's body or tomb counted as a relic in its own right; the word for one is *brandeum*. Popes used to send them as gifts, because a cloth could travel where a body couldn't be moved. If Gertrude's silk is one of those, it isn't wrapping anything. The silk is the relic, and the X-ray found exactly what it was meant to.

I can't tell which it is, and I don't think anyone handling the altar in 1045 or 1482 would have been asking. They had the label. Whoever wrote the name on that tag knew what was inside, or trusted whoever told them.

In 2020 the museum's textile conservator, Robin Hanson, made a new housing for each of the ten bundles, so they'd sit safely in the cavity without pressing on each other. Ten boxes, each fitted to its own bundle. Gertrude got one too, made to the shape of the silk, to go back into the altar with the others.
