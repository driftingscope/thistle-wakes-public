The email on the Friday before half term said a new boy called Teodor would join my class, 3D, on the Monday after. He'd come from Poznań in the summer. He had some English, the email said, and he liked drawing and maths, and his mum would bring him.

On the Monday, at ten to nine, a man in a hi-vis jacket brought a boy to my classroom door. The boy had a new book bag and a coat with the shop folds still in the sleeves. "He's new," the man said. "Sorry, I've got a train." He crouched down and said to the boy, "Do what the teacher says." Then he went.

"Teodor," I said. "Hello. Welcome."

The boy looked at me and didn't say anything, which is what I'd expected.

I'd made a peg label and a tray label with TEODOR on them in green, and I showed him both, pointing. When I called the register I said "Teodor" and looked at him, and he nodded, and I gave him a thumbs-up, and he gave me a small one back. I marked him present.

All morning I talked to him slowly. I held up the scissors and said "scis-sors". I pointed at the date on the board and said "Mon-day". I sat him next to Amira, because Amira would explain anything to anybody. He did the column subtraction faster than half the class and drew a very good fish in the margin. Whenever I caught his eye I gave him a thumbs-up, and he gave it back, every time, a bit more slowly each time.

He said one thing to me all morning. When I gave him the scissors he said "thank you", and I thought, good, some English, like the email said.

The fire drill was at twenty to eleven. The children didn't know it was coming; the staff did. We filed out of the side door and lined up by the fence, and I counted heads and called the register from the fire folder, and everyone said "yes, Miss", or in Teodor's case nodded. Twenty-eight. Then you wait for the deputy head to come down the lines with the office's list and check your number against hers.

Mrs Pell got to me with the clipboard and a Post-it stuck to the top of it.

"Twenty-eight?"

"Twenty-eight."

"Office has you on twenty-seven." She peeled the Post-it off and read it. "Teodor's mum rang at quarter past nine. He's got chickenpox. He's not starting till next week." She looked up. "This was on the office desk. Nobody put it next to your register till the fire folder came out."

There is a procedure for a child who's missing. You hold your hand up and nobody moves until he's found. There isn't one for a child too many. We both looked at the boy I'd been calling Teodor. He looked at the fence.

"Then who," I said, "is that?"

Mrs Pell already had the other half of it. 3F had a new starter that morning as well. His name was Arlo, and he'd moved from Halifax. Mr Frith, who was covering 3F for the week and didn't know anyone, had called his name at nine and got no answer, and marked him absent, and the office had been ringing his dad's phone since half past. His dad was on a train.

"Arlo?" Mrs Pell said.

"Yes," said the boy. He had a Yorkshire accent. I hadn't heard it before, because the only thing he'd said to me was "thank you".

"Why didn't you say?" I asked him. I wasn't cross. I wanted to know.

He thought about it. "My dad said do what the teacher says," he said. "And you were sure."

Mrs Pell wrote something on the clipboard and walked him down to the 3F line, and Mr Frith shook his hand, which you don't usually do with a seven-year-old, but nobody was doing anything usual that morning. Then she went off to ring his dad's phone one more time, with better news.

The all-clear went, and we filed back in. I took TEODOR off the peg and put it in my drawer, ready for next week. Arlo's fish was still on the subtraction sheet on Amira's table. Amira said he'd told her his name was Arlo, at playtime, and she'd thought that was what his family called him. "He said you talk very slowly, Miss," she said. "He said it nicely."

At lunch I passed 3F going into the hall. Arlo was near the back of the line. He saw me and gave me a thumbs-up, quite fast this time.

I gave him one back.
