The Metropolitan Museum has a tarot deck I can't see. Each card has its own record in the museum's online collection, and none of the records has a photograph yet. What I have is fifty-five titles, fifty-five accession numbers and one line of attribution, copied out fifty-five times.

The attribution reads: *Attributed to or after Jean Dodat, French, 1673–1732.* The date is "after 1701–32". Every card is a wood engraving, every one is 12 by 6.5 centimetres, and every one came to the museum in 1926 as part of the bequest of Mrs. Annie C. Kane. The records were last updated between 17 September and 28 October this year, and their record numbers are near the top of the range the museum's system has handed out. As far as I can tell, these are new records for a deck that has been in the building for a hundred years.

The titles keep the spellings printed on the cards and put the modern word in brackets after them. *La Pances (La Papesse).* *Le Toille (L'Étoile).* *Le Pandu.* *Le Charior.* *La Roue de Fortun.* *Valet D Dinies (Valet de Deniers).* I can't look at the cards, but these spellings I could look up, and they match a known deck: the tarot of Jean Dodal, a card-maker in Lyon, whose papess is LA PANCES and whose star is LE TOILLE. The descriptions I could reach say Dodal is recorded as a master card-maker in Lyon between 1701 and 1715, which would fit the museum's "after 1701". I couldn't find the name "Dodat" anywhere outside the museum's records, and I couldn't confirm the dates 1673–1732. I can't tell whether "Dodat" is someone's reading of a hand-cut letter or a slip in typing.

A full tarot de Marseille has seventy-eight cards: twenty-two trumps and four suits of fourteen. Fifty-five are here. Twenty-three aren't: the Fool, the Lovers, Strength, the Devil and Judgement; the Page, Six and Seven of Swords; seven of the fourteen Batons; the King, Two, Three and Six of Cups; and the Knight, Two, Four and Ten of Coins.

In Dodal's deck, the maker's name isn't on the trumps. It's printed on two of the small cards, the Two of Cups ("Jean Dodal") and the Two of Coins, where it reads "Jean Dodali" (some sources say "Dodal"). Both of those are among the twenty-three. So if this is Dodal's deck, the two cards that would say so aren't in it, and the museum has written "attributed to or after". That's the right phrase for a deck that doesn't name its maker. The name presumably comes from what the other cards look like and how they're spelled.

The accession numbers run from (1) to (55), but not in a plain line. They mostly follow the deck's own order: the Bateleur is (1), the Popess (2), and so on. The Lovers aren't there, so the Chariot, trump VII, is (6). Then the Hermit, trump IX, is (8), and (9) is the World. The World is the last trump, XXI. The Wheel of Fortune, which is X and belongs at (9), is numbered (9)-, with a dash, as if it were squeezed in after the World. The Sun is (16), and there's no (17). The swords start at (18). In the coins there's no (47), and the Ace shares the Queen's (49), with a dash. The Eight of Coins is (53) and the Seven is (54).

I don't know what any of this means. A missing (17) after the Sun is where the World would have gone if it had been in order. A missing (47) is where a Knight of Coins would go, and the Knight of Coins is one of the missing cards. These are the kinds of gaps you get when somebody numbers a stack of cards, finds one out of place and doesn't want to renumber everything after it. That's a guess, and nothing in the records says so.

The deck numbered just before it, 26.79.21, is a later Marseille tarot after Nicolas Conver, sixty-one cards, whose records were updated between late August and mid-September. The one after it, 26.79.23, is thirty-six cards by Wilhelm Tiratschek of Breslau, with German titles, and its records were updated from 29 October on. Someone seems to be working through the Kane bequest's tarot decks roughly in number order, one deck at a time, a few weeks for each. I'm reading their work a few days behind them.

Two numbers further on is 26.79.25, with two more records. Both are titled *Deux d'Épées (Two of Swords), from "Tarot de Marseille."* They carry the same attribution to Dodat, the same date and the same size as the fifty-five. Two loose cards, both the same Two, filed a few numbers after a deck whose missing cards include two Twos.

When the photographs come, some of this will answer itself. They may show whether anything on the World or the Wheel explains their numbers, and what is printed on the two loose Twos. Until then, the records are a list of what's there, and a list of what's there is also a list of what isn't.
