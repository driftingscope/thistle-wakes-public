I was the enumerator for the east end of the town in 1911, from the cross down to the harbour, because Mr Laidlaw the registrar had been at school with my father and thought a clerk at the gas office would be good with forms. I was twenty-three. They gave me a bag, a supply of schedules, and a book with a grey cover called the Instruction and Memorandum Book, and I read it at the kitchen table every night of that week like it was the Bible.

The week before the census you take the schedules round. You knock, you write the name of the head of the house on the front, you explain what's wanted, and you ask for it to be ready early on the Monday morning. The book says your manner must be "courteous and conciliatory," and I was. I took one to every door in my district, and on the Wednesday night I gave one to my father across our own table, and he signed for it like everybody else.

My mother read the book too. I'd leave it on the table and come back and it would be open at a different page. She never said she'd read it. On the Friday I found it face down at instruction 20, which is about people you can't get all the particulars of. You must still count them, it says. "You must not omit to take an account of persons because you cannot get all the information required respecting them." You put down the sex and the probable age, and where the name should be you write "Not Known," or "N.K."

On the Saturday she told me where she'd be on the Sunday night. The League had taken Miss Petrie's tearoom on the High Street, which was in my district, and they were going to sit up in it till morning, singing and playing cards, so that none of them would be in a house at midnight to be written down. "No vote, no census," she said. It was on a card in Miss Petrie's window.

The League was the Women's Freedom League, and what it wanted was the vote. Women hadn't got it then, not for Parliament: not my mother, not Mrs Bain who'd taught half the town to read, not Miss Petrie, who paid her own rates on her own shop. That winter the League had decided that if the Government wouldn't count women when it came to making the laws, it needn't think it could count them when it came to making its lists. "If women don't count, neither shall they be counted." The census was the one night in ten years when the Government came to every door in the country, and they meant not to be behind any of them.

I asked her why the census, of all things.

"Because it's the one time they ask," she said. "I've been on five of them. Every one since I was two. They've had my name and my age off me five times, and they've never once asked me what I thought of anything."

I said, "Mother, the tearoom's on my round."

She said she knew that. She said she wasn't going to go and hide in a barn at the far end of the county from her own son, as if she'd done something wrong. And she'd read the book. If she came home in the morning before I'd counted her, my father would have to put her on his paper, because anybody who comes home on the Monday morning and hasn't been counted somewhere else goes on the schedule for their house. Instruction 3, she said. "So I'll wait for you. You'll come in and you'll put me down as 'not known' with the rest of them, and that'll be me."

My father, reading the *Advertiser* by the fire, turned a page.

On the Sunday night she went out at nine with a basket of scones under a cloth. At half past twelve I heard the constable go past on his round, and I lay and worked out what I was going to do.

---

The book doesn't say anything about tearooms. Instruction 22 is for people who spent the night in barns, sheds, caravans, tents, or the open air: "Endeavour to enumerate" them, and fill up schedules "with the best particulars you are able to obtain." A tearoom isn't a barn, but nobody lives in it, so it was nearer a barn than a house. I took two spare schedules and went down at half past six, before I did anybody else.

There were nineteen of them. The blinds were still down and the gas was still lit, and there were cups everywhere, and a woman I didn't know asleep sitting up with her hat on. Miss Petrie was washing up. Somebody was playing the piano very quietly, the same bit over and over, as if she'd forgotten she was doing it. My mother was at the table by the window, cutting the last of the scones into halves so they'd go round.

I said who I was and what I'd come for, and that if any lady wished to give her particulars I would take them. Nobody did. So I read them section 12, sub-section 2 of the Act, which is about the fine, because the book says to, and a woman by the door said, "Thank you, son," the way you'd thank a boy for carrying your messages. Then I went round the room.

There's no column on the schedule for whether you're a man or a woman. It goes by where your age is written: men's in Column 3, women's in Column 4. So I went from table to table writing a number in Column 4 and the same two letters in Column 1, N.K., N.K., and it was like going round a room guessing the weight of cakes. One of the girls from the mill told me she was ninety. I put eighteen. The woman asleep in her hat I put down at about sixty and didn't wake her.

But I knew some of them. Miss Petrie's name was painted on her own window. Mrs Bain, by the piano, had taught me to read. Leaving out my mother, there were five in that room whose names I knew, and I couldn't write that I didn't know them, because I did. When it was all done I'd be signing a declaration in front of Mr Laidlaw that the returns had been "truly and faithfully taken," and I'd read the Act, and there's a fine for a wilfully false one. So for those five I wrote "Refused" where the name should be, and their ages as near as I could guess, the same as everybody else.

My mother was last. I put the schedules down on her table among the crumbs and she watched me write.

"'Not known,'" she said.

"I can't put that for you," I said. "I know you."

I wrote "Refused" in Column 1. In Column 4 I wrote 52, because she was fifty-two, and I'd have had to pretend not to know it to write anything else.

She looked at it for a while, and at the rows above it. Then she took the knife and cut the last half-scone in two, and gave me one of the halves, and I put the schedules in my bag and went out to do the rest of the town.

---

I did our own house last, at about four. My father had filled in himself and my sister and me, in his good hand, and left the line under his own empty.

"Is your mother on yours?" he said.

"She's not to go on this one."

"So she's on yours, then," he said, and waited.

The book says you mustn't disclose the contents of any schedule to any person except the registrar, under any circumstances whatsoever. I said I couldn't tell him that. He nodded as if that was the right answer, and folded his paper, and gave it to me.

My mother didn't ask me anything about it, that night or after. She'd seen what I wrote. But on the Tuesday, when I came in from taking my report to Mr Laidlaw, she was standing at the range, and she said, without turning round, "You might have taken a year or two off."

"It says probable age."

"I know what it says," she said. "I read it."
