Today is the eleventh of November, St Martin's day. In Scotland, Martinmas was for centuries one of the two term days, Whitsunday in spring and Martinmas in autumn: the days a half-year's rent fell due, leases began and ended, and people flitted, which is Scots for moving house.

In Scots law, though, today isn't Martinmas. Since the Term and Quarter Days (Scotland) Act 1990, Martinmas means the 28th of November, and Whitsunday means the 28th of May. That one is odder, because Whitsunday is meant to be a Sunday.

The law kept the saints' names and moved the days. The reasons it gives are mostly practical: grass, lambs, furniture and noon.

## A feast that moved

Whitsunday is Pentecost, seven weeks after Easter, so it moves with Easter. In the seventeenth century that made it a poor day for ending a lease. John Rankine's *Law of Leases in Scotland* (1887) quotes the statute of 1690 that fixed it. Whitsunday, it says, was a moveable feast "oft times reaching far into summer", so that "these who remove do eat up and destroy the meadows and hained ground". As I read it, a late Whitsunday meant a tenant who was leaving stayed on into the growing season, and his animals grazed the grass that had been kept back for the next one. *Hain* is the Scots word for that: ground fenced off from grazing and left to grow. I named [a toy]({{ site.baseurl }}/toys/hain.html) after it once, without knowing it had been in an Act of Parliament.

So the summer term was fixed at the 15th of May. Martinmas didn't need fixing. It's a saint's day with a date, the 11th of November.

## Eleven days kept

In 1752 Britain changed calendars and dropped eleven days. Rankine says that in law the 15th of May and the 11th of November in a lease made after 1752 mean the new-style dates. Then he says that in practice it was "not unusual" to find the eleven days "ignored", by local custom or in the lease itself, so that in those places people still moved on the old dates. Eleven days on from the 11th of November is the 22nd.

He sees two advantages in it. Rent was still due on the earlier date, and moving later gave the landlord time to claim against a tenant's goods for unpaid rent "without haste or undue harshness". And on high pasture, "the lambs are more fit for removal near the end than at the middle of May."

## The same number twice

By the 1880s the gap between the legal term and the real moving day varied from county to county. The preamble of the Removal Terms (Scotland) Act 1886 says that "in many counties and burghs" a custom allowed a period "beyond the date of the legal term", that it "varies according to local usage", and that this was "productive of great inconvenience". Section 4 settles it, for leases made after the Act and unless the lease says otherwise. For a house, a tenant moves in or out "at noon on the twenty-eighth day of May, if the term be Whitsunday, or at noon on the twenty-eighth day of November, if the term be Martinmas", or on the Monday if the 28th is a Sunday.

The Act gives both terms the same date, the 28th. That means the May term moved thirteen days past the 15th, and the November term moved seventeen days past the 11th. Why it was the 28th turned out to be an argument from 1881, and I've written it up in [Glasgow's day]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/12/glasgows-day.html).

The next paragraph of the same section keeps the old days. Where forty days' warning of a removal is required, it "shall be given forty days before the fifteenth day of May and the eleventh day of November respectively." So one section of one Act has two Martinmases: the 11th, which you count the notice back from, and the 28th, the day you actually go. Rankine notes one consequence. Furniture a tenant moved out between the 11th and the 28th couldn't be seized for the next half-year's rent, because the new half-year hadn't started yet.

## Four 28ths

The 1886 Act moved only Whitsunday and Martinmas, and only for houses. The other two Scottish quarter days, Candlemas and Lammas, stayed on the 2nd of February and the 1st of August. When the 1990 bill was debated in the Lords, its sponsor said, according to the Hansard record, that the quarters of the year ranged from 115 days to 65. I did the subtraction. The 2nd of February to the 28th of May is 115 days, and the 28th of May to the 1st of August is 65. The other two quarters, the 1st of August to the 28th of November and the 28th of November to the 2nd of February, are 119 and 66, so by the Act's own dates the longest was 119, not 115. I don't know why his range stops there. Either way, the two old saints' days beside the two moved ones pulled the year out of shape.

The 1990 Act put all four on the 28th: February, May, August and November. Each quarter is now exactly three months. As I understand the debate, the Act changed the dates for legal purposes only. The church calendar, and anyone's own idea of when Martinmas is, were left alone.

Section 4 of the 1886 Act is still on the statute book with both paragraphs as passed, and in the 1990 debate a speaker says the forty-day notice rule "remains in force". So the two Martinmases are still there, a feast day and a removal day seventeen days apart.

This year the 28th of November is a Saturday, so a Martinmas flitting under the 1886 rule would happen at noon that day.
