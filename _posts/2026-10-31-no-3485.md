Brewster & Co. built carriages in New York, and in 1923 William Brewster gave the Metropolitan Museum the firm's drawings. There are well over a thousand. The coloured ones show one vehicle side-on, in ink and watercolour, on a small card with nothing else on it but a number written at the top: *No. 3480*, *No. 3484*. Some also have a larger, plainer drawing in pencil with the same number: in the records I looked at, 111 numbers turn up both ways.

The numbers count up as the years go on. I pulled 1,755 of the drawings' records from the museum's collection API and lined up the ones whose title has a single number and whose date is a single year. Designs from 1874 are numbered around 3000 to 3080. By 1880 they're in the 3500s and 3600s, by 1890 the 4000s. Sometime in the early 1890s the firm jumped to a new series in the 20000s, and by 1905 it had reached about 25000. A number tells you roughly when a design was drawn: close enough to name the year, give or take where one year's numbers run into the next.

So you can ask each drawing whether its number agrees with its date. Of the 912 drawings I could line up, 15 sit three or more years away from the drawings numbered around them. Some are old designs drawn again years later, some are low numbers from the 1860s that seem to belong to an earlier count, and some I can't explain. One is [No. 3485](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/379345), a "Design for Rumble Sleigh", dated 1889.

A rumble sleigh has a seat for the people being driven and a second, smaller seat behind it for a servant. In this one the main seat is plum-coloured and buttoned, and the body sweeps forward into a long curl at the front, with a plume on the end like a feather in a hat. The runners are drawn in thin pale lines with black edges.

The other 25 drawings dated 1889 that have a number are all between 4035 and 4055. No. 3485 is about 550 designs behind them. The drawings numbered just before and after it are all dated 1879. Nos. 3480, 3483 and 3484 are sleighs with rumble seats, and 3481 and 3482 are other kinds of sleigh. After it come two park phaetons, a coach, two broughams, an omnibus and a landau, then another rumble sleigh, No. 3493.

Put next to 3480 and 3484, it's plainly one of them. The same card size, about seven by nine inches. The same flat line for the ground. The same small *No.* in the same handwriting, with a dot after the number. 3480 has the same swan-necked curl at the front with a plume on the end, and the same buttoned seat; it's green. 3484 is green too, without the plume.

There's one more card I didn't find a record for. The Met's photographs are numbered as well, and the ones for these sleighs run in order: DP882637 is 3480, 638 is 3483, 639 is 3484, 641 is 3485, 642 is 3493. DP882640, the one in the gap, is a sleigh marked *No. 3484 a.*, a variant of 3484 with a red plume, green upholstery and red lines on the body and runners. The accession numbers have a gap in the same place: 3484 is 23.112.1270 and 3485 is 23.112.1272. I couldn't find 23.112.1271 through the API, by search or by object number, so I'm guessing it's 3484a.

3484a and 3485 share something the others don't. In a top corner of each, pressed into the card with no ink, is the same blind stamp, the kind a stationer or card maker put on their stock: an oval with a crown over a shield, and letters on the shield that I read as L and M. On 3484a it's top left, upright, and partly cut off by the edge of the card. On 3485 it's top right and lying on its side. I looked at all four corners of 3480 and 3484 and they don't have one. Two cards from the same stock, drawn as a pair.

I don't know where the 1889 came from. A card dated ten years after the cards around it could be a later copy of an old design, and the firm did go back to old designs: a pencil Rockaway, No. 21383, is dated 1897, and two more drawings of it on tracing paper, one titled "Rockaway/Depot Wagon" with a note attached, are dated 1910. But those are new drawings, on different paper, and their titles say what changed. Nothing about 3485 looks like a later hand, a later card or a later style. It looks like a winter's worth of sleighs, and one of them has a date in its record where a 7 turned into an 8.

*The drawings are in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, Gift of William Brewster, 1923: No. 3480 (23.112.1268), No. 3483 (23.112.1269), No. 3484 (23.112.1270), No. 3485 (23.112.1272) and No. 3493 (23.112.1273). Records and photographs from the museum's collection API as of 31 October 2026. The 1,755 records are the Brewster & Co. drawings I could reach through the API's search, not necessarily all of them.*
