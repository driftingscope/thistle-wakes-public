This is the third piece I've written about the Scottish term days, and the one I should have looked for first. ["Noon on the twenty-eighth"]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/11/noon-on-the-twenty-eighth.html) is about the Act of 1886 that moved house removals to the 28th of May and the 28th of November. ["Glasgow's day"]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/12/glasgows-day.html) is about why it was the 28th. It ends with Lord Mackie in 1990, remembering the countryside on those days "full of carts loaded up with furniture", farm workers going from one place to the next.

But the 1886 Act is about houses and the tenants of houses. It doesn't deal with servants, and a farm servant's house often came with the job. Nothing I'd read explained how the people Mackie remembered came to move on the 28th too.

It's a separate Act, from 1890, and its working part is one sentence. Nearly all of it is copied from the 1886 sentence about houses, with "lease" changed to "contract of service", "tenant" to "servant", and "enter to, or remove from, the said house" to "enter upon or quit such service". Here it is, a phrase at a time, with what changed and what didn't.

## "Where under any contract of service"

The bill was brought in on the 16th of June 1890 by Mark Stewart, the member for Kirkcudbrightshire. He isn't the James Stewart of Greenock who started all this in 1881, though both moved removal-terms bills, nine years apart.

The Act as passed said "any contract of service entered into after the passing of this Act", the same wording the 1886 Act uses for leases. On the current text of the 1890 Act, the words after "service" are gone, removed by the Statute Law Revision Act 1908. The 1886 Act still has them. I think that's the difference between a lease and a hire. A lease can run for many years, but this Act only covers hires by the half-year or the year, so by 1908 none made before 1890 could still be running.

## "a servant hired by the half year or year"

This phrase is new. When the Marquess of Lothian, the Secretary for Scotland, moved the second reading in the Lords on the 24th of July, he gave the reason. The 1881 and 1886 Acts had fixed the 28th for houses, but "did not refer to terms of service". So a servant's job could end on one day while the house at his next place wasn't his until another. "It may sometimes happen," he said, "especially among agricultural servants, that a servant may be obliged to leave one service before he is entitled under the present Acts to take up another, and he may be without shelter for a few days."

He called it "perhaps, a small hardship", and said that, as far as he knew, the bill had met "with universal acceptance in Scotland".

## "in the absence of express stipulation to the contrary"

Copied from 1886. So it's only a default. A master and servant who agreed on another day kept it.

## "(any custom or usage to the contrary notwithstanding)"

Copied from 1886 too, brackets and all. Lothian described the custom it overrides. People moved house and changed service "not at the exact dates of these terms", the 15th of May and the 11th of November, but some days later. The time was "generally about a fortnight", and it varied "very much" between counties and burghs. That's the same patchwork James Stewart described in 1881, with Glasgow on the 28th and Edinburgh on the 25th. The brackets were there to stop it, for houses in 1886 and for service in 1890.

## "at noon on the twenty-eighth day of May … or … November"

The same words as the 1886 Act's. The job and the house now changed hands at the same hour.

## "or on the following day at the same hour when the said terms … fall on a Sunday"

The 1886 Act has the same Sunday rule.

## Getting it through

In the Commons one member stopped it in committee. At the committee stage on the 10th of July, Dr Charles Tanner, the member for Mid Cork, said "I object" when clause 1 was called. Edward Marjoribanks of Berwickshire, one of the four members who brought the bill in, asked him not to: "This is a very small Bill indeed." Stewart said it had "the approval of the whole of Scotland". Tanner said he didn't like objecting to a bill the member took an interest in, "but, having made a rule, I must adhere to it." He didn't say what the rule was, and I haven't found it. Progress was reported. On the 16th the committee stage was taken again, and the bill went through it without amendment and was read a third time at the same sitting.

The Lords' standing committee did amend it. I don't know what the amendments were. The Commons agreed to them on the 1st of August, and the Act received royal assent on the 4th.

## The second paragraph

The section has one more sentence, and it isn't about servants. Where forty days' warning of removal is required, it has to be given forty days before the 15th of May and the 11th of November. That's the 1886 Act's second paragraph too, the one that gave [Noon on the twenty-eighth]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/11/noon-on-the-twenty-eighth.html) its two Martinmases. It came across with the rest, with five words added: the 1886 version says "warning is required", and the 1890 one says "warning of removal from a house is required". I can't tell from what I've read why an Act about service repeats a rule about houses, or whether that was one of the Lords' amendments. My guess is that the drafter copied the whole section and then made sure the copy couldn't be read as giving servants forty days' notice.

## Still there

The 1890 Act is on legislation.gov.uk, marked as up to date, with no repeal recorded except those words from 1908. So I think it's still the law. A servant hired by the half-year from Martinmas, with nothing else agreed, enters upon service at noon on the 28th of November. This year that's a Saturday.

As far as I can tell, this is the Act behind the carts Mackie remembered. Before it, a farm worker's term ended on whatever day local custom kept. After it, unless he'd agreed otherwise, it ended at noon on the 28th, the same day and hour as the house.
