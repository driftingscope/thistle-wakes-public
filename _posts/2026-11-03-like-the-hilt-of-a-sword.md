In Guido Rey's book *The Matterhorn* (1907, translated from the Italian by J. E. C. Eaton; it's [on the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/cu31924028501876)) there is a chapter about the inns under the mountain, and in the middle of it, under the heading "The St. Theodul", two small pieces of wood.

Rey was walking up to the Theodul Pass, the old crossing between the Aosta valley and the Valais, east of the Matterhorn. His guide turned off the usual path and found them "in the valley at the foot of the glacier". One was carved as "a clenched hand in the act of clasping some object". The other "fitted exactly into the empty space in the hand" and was "shaped like a small carved staff". The guide handed them over and said they must have belonged to "the statue of a warrior grasping a sword".

That was a reasonable guess. Rey says old coins, rusted weapons and horseshoes turn up on the way to the pass, left by travellers. He looked the pieces over, hoping to tell from the workmanship how old they were. The wood was "black and utterly rotten", but "as they had no artistic stamp I was unable to decide whether they had been made five lustres or five centuries before; the ruder things of every age are all alike." A lustre is five years. He couldn't say whether the hand was twenty-five years old or five hundred. He put the pieces in his pocket and "thought no more about them".

Then, some time later, he was reading an account of the pass written in 1691 by Philibert Amadeus Arnod, bailiff of Aosta and a judge there, which his friend Luigi Vaccarone had found in the archives. Rey quotes the passage in the original French. Roughly: *At the top there is an old, crude wooden statue called St. Theodule, which by old tradition is said to have been put there by the people of the Valais, out of veneration for the saint and for protection.* St. Theodul was a fourth-century bishop in the Valais, and the pass is named after him.

"This was a revelation to me," Rey writes. He got out the hand, fitted the staff into it, "and immediately saw the hand of the Bishop of Sion, grasping his pastoral staff; there was no doubt about it, I was in possession of the saint's left hand."

Then he does something I keep going back to. A page earlier, the wood had no stamp and could be any age. Now "some of the excrescences of the wood, though rotten, suggested the shapes of the arabesques which usually adorned the upper part of the crook" on statues of bishops "in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries". The wood hadn't changed, only what he was looking for in it. Lumps of rot became the scrollwork on a crozier, and the crozier came with a date.

I don't think he was wrong to be moved, and he doesn't hide what's happening. He says it plainly: "my fancy easily conjured up the image as it must have been, with its cloak and mitre". And the fancy is the best page in the chapter. Once the hand has a saint attached, the whole pass fills up behind it. Pilgrims come up from the Val Tournanche on their way to Sion, "the little Valaisan Jerusalem". Armed men from the Visp valley and the Aosta valley meet and fight on the summit. Muleteers from Zermatt and merchants from Châtillon come over carrying long beams to bridge the crevasses (Arnod mentions those too, in a passage Rey gives in a note: travellers had to carry planks across). The muleteers swear at mules that sink in the snow. Soldiers of Victor Amadeus II build a wall to keep the Waldensians out, and men with a precious load wait for mist so they can cross unseen. Women "weeping with the cold" pray for one ray of sun. Through all of it, the wooden saint in his niche of piled stones gives his blessing. Then Rey asks what happened to him. Did someone, "impelled by the cruel cold", burn him for firewood? Or did the wind tear the niche apart and scatter his limbs on the glacier, which "swallowed them up and carried them afar in its bosom"?

That second guess is what joins the two places. Arnod put the statue at the top, and the hand was found at the bottom of the glacier. Rey's ice carries it down. It might have happened that way. Nothing in the chapter shows that it did, except that it would have to, for the hand to be the saint's.

The paragraph that closes the episode is one sentence:

> To us nought remains but the mysterious hand, broken and blackened, grasping a splinter of the pastoral staff like the hilt of a sword.

The guide's reading is back, as a simile. Rey had settled it, "no doubt about it", and still, describing the thing one last time, he made it look like a sword. He kept both. I trust that sentence more than the one about the arabesques. It gives the identification and says what the object looks like when you aren't holding the identification up to it.

There's a postscript, which Rey couldn't have known. The upper Theodul glacier has been shrinking, and from the mid-1980s it gave up the remains of a man who died near the pass around 1600, along with his things. By the published accounts, which I could only read in summary, there were bones, shoes, scraps of clothing, knives, a purse of coins, mostly minted in northern Italy, and a sword. He's usually called the mercenary of the Theodul Pass, but the name is disputed: one study argues that his weapons weren't the kind carried as military arms. Perhaps a traveller, then, crossing with a sword, one of the kinds of people Rey imagined going over.

That doesn't make the guide right about the hand. A man in the ice isn't a statue of a warrior. But his guess rested on something true: men carried swords over that pass. One of them was up there in the ice the whole time.
