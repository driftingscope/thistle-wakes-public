*A short story. Made up, like the others.*

In 2013 Josie spent four days in a booth in Leeds reading out the names of railway stations. She said each one twice: once rising, for the middle of a sentence, *The next station is*, and once falling, for the end, *This train terminates at*. A computer would join the pieces up later. She was paid by the day and asked to sound the same on the fourth afternoon as on the first morning, which is harder than it sounds.

The script had a column for pronunciation. Most of it was empty, because most names say what they mean. Where it wasn't, someone had typed things like LUFF-bruh and KEETH-lee. Next to Coldhithe there was nothing. She pressed the talkback and asked the engineer, a young man called Stu who had been eating crisps for four days, and he looked down at his copy and said, "As spelt, I'd say." So she said it as spelt. Cold, hithe. Rising, then falling.

Thirteen years later the train company was replacing its announcement system, and the new one needed thirty-one names done again. Tamsin, who produced for the agency now, sent the list over. It had a column headed *Correct pronunciation* and one headed *Notes*. Beside Coldhithe it said COLL-ij, and in the notes: *Local press, conflicting. Cuttings attached for info.*

The cuttings were two scans from the letters page of the *Coldhithe and District Courier*. The first letter was from Dennis Ashby, Station Adopter, Coldhithe.

> Sir, — I see the railway is to replace its announcements. The town is called Collidge. It has been called Collidge for as long as anybody has been writing it down wrong. I have looked after the planters on platform one since 2011, and every train that stops tells my visitors they have arrived somewhere else. In 2015 the railway wrote to say the matter would be reviewed when the system was next updated. I trust it now will be.

The second was from the following week, under the heading *Station name: a younger view*.

> Sir, — My name is Hattie Ashby and I am 12. My grandad wrote to you last week about the station. Please don't let them change it. Most people at my school moved here for the new houses, and the first thing that told them the name was the train, so that is what everyone says now. Only my grandad and people his age say Collidge, and Dad when he isn't thinking. When you say it you have to do the little stop in the middle like the train does, Cold . . . hithe, or it doesn't count. I know it is spelt wrong for Collidge but it isn't spelt wrong for Cold-hithe.

Josie read them both twice. She didn't remember a stop. She had said eleven hundred names that week and tried to say every one of them the same way, at the same speed, and the whole point was that a passenger shouldn't be able to hear where one recording ended and the next began.

Tamsin rang that afternoon. "You've seen the Ashbys."

"I've seen them. What about the girl?"

"What about her? It's not our call, and it's not the paper's. The railway goes by the gazetteer, and the gazetteer says Collidge."

"I know," Josie said. And she did know. She had never been to Coldhithe. She couldn't have found it on a map without looking.

---

The new session was in a different studio, in Manchester, with an engineer called Abi who drank only hot water. The thirty-one names took under an hour. Most of the corrections were stresses in the wrong place. She asked to leave Coldhithe until last.

"Collidge," she said, rising. Then falling: "Collidge." It was an easy word. It went straight through, with nothing in the middle.

"Lovely," said Tamsin from behind the glass. "Clean. That's us."

"Have you got the old one?" Josie said. "The 2013 one. Can I hear it?"

Abi had to go and find it. The old recordings were in an archive folder on a server, under the operator's name and a date, and every name was its own short file. She found *Coldhithe_rise* and *Coldhithe_fall* and played the first one into the booth.

It was Josie's voice at thirty-one, a little higher than now, and very even. *Cold* — and then a gap, not long, less than a breath, so short that Josie would not have heard it if she had not been told to listen for it — *hithe*.

"Play the other one," she said.

The falling one had it too. *Cold*, the same small hold, then *hithe*, dropping away to the end of a sentence.

"I can tighten that if it's bothering you," said Abi. "It's a fifth of a second, if that. It could easily be an edit."

"It isn't an edit," Josie said.

She knew what it was, now that she heard it. She had read *Cold* without thinking, because it was a word, and then she had come to *hithe*, which she had never said aloud in her life, and for a moment she had checked it before she said it. That was the stop. It was her, thirteen years ago in a booth in Leeds, not sure of her footing. The trains had played it to a girl in Coldhithe for her whole life, every time one came in, and the girl had decided it was part of the word.

"Leave it," she said. "It's not going out anyway."

On the train home she looked it up. A hithe is a landing place on a river, somewhere small enough to pull a boat up. From the spring, the trains would say Collidge, cleanly, all the way through, and Dennis Ashby's visitors would arrive where they meant to. Hattie would go on saying it with the stop in it, and she would never know that the stop was a woman in Leeds who had never seen the word before.

---

*Coldhithe is made up. The re-recording isn't: in 2023 the train operator Northern re-recorded more than thirty station names after asking people who lived there how to say them. One was Slaithwaite, in West Yorkshire, which the old recording called "slaith-waite". The people who live there say "slou-wit".*
