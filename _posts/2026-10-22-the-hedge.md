## September

I heard the trimmer before I saw him. It was a Saturday, early, the kind of early that's meant as a courtesy to nobody.

The hedge runs across the bottom of both our gardens, eleven metres of privet between us and the railway. By the time I'd got my dressing gown on and gone to the back window, he'd done the whole face of his half, cut straight down, the way they look in the catalogues for things you don't need. There were clippings all over his lawn in a pale green fan. He was standing on a stepladder in the far corner with the trimmer held out in front of him, looking along the top like a man sighting a rifle.

Then he got down, and moved the ladder, and did the top, flat as a table, working towards the middle. The fence between our gardens stops at the two sheds, a couple of metres short of the hedge. I'd have thought he'd go by that. When he'd finished there was a step in the top of the hedge halfway along, not quite where the fence would have put it, his half square and pale, my half the dark, tufted thing it's been since Easter.

From my side it looked like a haircut someone had walked out of halfway through.

I didn't go out. I'd met him twice. Once when he moved in, in July, when I took round a card and he said thank you very much and held it in both hands as if he wasn't sure whether to open it in front of me. And once at the bins, when we both said it was warm.

He swept the clippings into green sacks, eight of them. He put the ladder in his shed. He went in.

I made tea and drank it standing at the window. The thing about the step was that you could see exactly where he'd decided my hedge began. It was a good guess. Probably better than mine. I've always thought of the hedge as mostly mine, because I was here first and because Frank planted it, all the way across, the year we came; the people at 16 then were glad of anything that kept out the trains. But if you'd asked me where the line ran I'd have said somewhere in the middle and waved my hand.

He hadn't waved his hand. He'd gone along his whole half, on a ladder, holding a machine at arm's length, and stopped at the point where he thought he ought to stop.

I could see two ways of taking it. One was that he was being polite: he'd done what was his and hadn't presumed. The other was that he was being exact, the way you're exact with somebody when you'd like them to notice.

I had the same shears I'd had for years and a stepladder that wobbles on grass. I went and looked at both in the garage, and then I came back in.

On Monday there was a note through the door. It was on squared paper, folded twice.

*Hi, it's Tunde from 16. Sorry about the noise on Saturday. I did my side of the hedge. I can do yours too if you like, I've got the trimmer out anyway. No problem if not.*

I put it on the fridge under the magnet from Whitby. I didn't answer it that day. I wanted to decide first which way to take it.

## October

I answered the note at the start of October, nearly three weeks late, and I wrote it on the back of his own note because I had no squared paper and lined seemed wrong.

*Thank you, that's kind of you. I'll do my own.*

I'd decided he was being exact. I wasn't sure I believed it. But if he was only being polite there was nothing to do about it, and if he was being exact I could answer him.

I started on the Sunday. The sycamore at 18 keeps the morning sun off his end, so my half grows faster than his, though that isn't something you can say to a neighbour without it sounding like a complaint about somebody else's tree. The face took me three afternoons with the shears, standing on the lawn, doing as high as I could reach and then stopping. My arms were no good by four o'clock. Each time I came in and looked at it from the window, and each time it was wavier than it had felt.

The top needed the ladder. On the fourth afternoon I set it up in my corner and climbed to the second step, and it sank on one side into the lawn, the way it always has, and tipped me towards the hedge. I got my balance with a hand on the top step and went on.

I was cutting along towards the middle when someone knocked on my side gate.

It was him. He said, "Sorry. I saw your ladder go, from the kitchen." He looked at it. "Can I hold that?"

I said he could, and came down and drew the bolt for him, and he held the ladder. He held it with both hands and kept his eyes on my feet, not on the hedge.

He didn't offer to do the cutting. I'd thought he might, and I'd had an answer ready, and I didn't need it.

We moved along the hedge a metre at a time. I asked him how he'd found the middle, because I'd been wondering for a month.

"I counted the trunks," he said. "There are twenty-nine."

"I'd have gone by the fence."

"The fence wanders," he said. "You can see it from upstairs."

"So which one's the middle?"

"The fifteenth. I did half of it."

I looked down through the hedge at the fifteenth trunk. Frank planted them the first spring we were here, and I held the other end of the string. I had never counted them. It had not crossed my mind that there might be an odd number, and that someone would one day have to decide what to do about the one in the middle.

I'd have said it was mine, if anyone had asked. I'd have been wrong by half a trunk.

When we got to it I could see where his cut had grown on in the month since, a few pale shoots standing up off his flat top at the step. I cut my half down to the level of his, as well as I could get it. Then, without deciding to, I reached over and took the shoots off his half as well, three or four snips, and let them drop.

He didn't say anything about that. He held the ladder while I came down, folded it for me, and carried it to the garage. I said thank you and he said no problem. Then he went back out by the side gate and I bolted it after him.

From the back window, later, I saw him come down his garden and stand at the hedge, at the fifteenth trunk, and look along the top. I don't know which way he took it. I hadn't decided which way I meant it.

## November

Frank comes in November to do the gutters. He has done it every year since he moved out, which is twelve years now. I could pay somebody. He says it's no trouble, and it's a way of seeing each other that doesn't have to be called anything.

He brings his own ladder, the long aluminium one, because he doesn't trust mine, although he's the one who bought it.

He was up at the back of the house by eleven, scooping sycamore leaves out of the gutter into a carrier bag hung on the ladder. I stood on the patio and held the bottom of it, because I always do, and because he is seventy-one.

"Can you do it a bit quieter," I said. "He works nights."

"Who does?"

"Next door. Tunde." I nodded at the back bedroom window of 16, which is about four feet from our gutter, with its curtains drawn at eleven in the morning. "His car comes in at seven. The curtains are shut till three."

Frank looked at the curtains, and then down at me, and went on scooping, a little more quietly.

When he got to the corner he stopped and looked down the garden. From up there you can see the whole top of the hedge, the way you can't from the ground. I'd only seen it from the back bedroom.

"You've had that done," he said.

"He did his side in September. I did mine."

"You did the top?"

"With the shears. Off the stepladder. He held it for me."

Frank looked along the hedge for a long time. I'd done my best to match the height of Tunde's half, and from the ground I'd thought I had. From Frank's face I could see that I hadn't, quite.

"He's halved the middle one," he said.

"He counted. There are twenty-nine."

"I know there are. I told you that when we put them in. I said, it's an odd number, somebody'll have to have the middle one one day." He shook the bag. "You were holding the string."

I didn't remember him saying it. I remembered the string, and the cold, and his back, and a lot of talk about the depth of the trench. I suppose it was in there.

He came down a few rungs and stopped again, and looked down the garden at it.

"He's telling you something," he said.

He'd have been the one to know. In twenty-two years Frank never once told me he minded something. He'd do it again himself, properly, and leave it where I'd see. If I pegged the washing out he'd go and re-peg it, every shirt by the side seams. The first summer I had it out with him, more than once. He said pegging by the collar pulls the neck out of shape, and that was all he would ever say. After that I let him, and he went on doing it every week, and I'd look out of the window and know exactly what he thought, and that it wasn't about collars.

"He held my ladder," I said.

"That one?" said Frank. He laughed. He moved his ladder along to the next length of gutter and went up again, and I held the bottom of it.

He went at two. He had a cup of tea first and told me the downpipe at the front was cracked and he'd bring a new section next time, which might mean next week and might mean next November.

## December

It snowed in the second week of December, the wet kind that comes down in lumps and sits where it lands. It started at about eight, an hour after his car came in, and by ten the garden had gone quiet under it.

From the back bedroom I could see the whole top of the hedge. My half was taking the snow the way it always had, in tufts, and letting some of it slide off down the sides. His half was holding all of it. It had furred over since September, but it was still flat, and the snow lay on it like a cloth, getting thicker, and in two or three places the top had started to open, the branches leaning outwards under the weight.

The curtains next door were shut. They'd be shut till three.

I found the soft broom in the garage and put my wellingtons on. From the lawn I could just reach the top with the broom held at the very end of the handle. I started in my corner and pulled the snow off the top towards me, a metre at a time, on to the lawn. It came off in slabs, and the branches came back up after it, slowly, not all the way.

It was hard on the arms in a different way from the shears. Snow kept getting into my collar. Behind the sheds there's nothing to stop at, and I didn't stop at the fifteenth trunk. I'd gone a metre past it before I thought of it, and then I went on along his half to the far corner.

It went on snowing, so at one o'clock I did it again.

At ten past three the curtains opened. I was in the kitchen, and I saw him come down his garden in a coat over what looked like pyjamas, and stand by the hedge, and look along the clear top, and at the snow heaped along the bottom of it on his lawn, and at my boot prints going along behind his shed. Then he went back up his garden and in, and came round by the front, and knocked on my side gate.

I drew the bolt. He had snow on his shoulders already.

"Was that you?"

"It was opening up along the top," I said. "It does that to flat ones."

He looked at the hedge. "I didn't know that."

"Frank used to say so." He didn't know who Frank was, and I didn't explain.

He stood there a moment longer. Then he said, "In October, when you did those bits on my side." He stopped. "I wondered if I'd done it wrong. The middle."

I thought about the shoots I'd taken off his half, three or four snips, and let drop on his lawn. Then I thought about Frank's shirts on my washing line, every one moved to the side seams, and how I'd always known exactly what he meant by it.

"No," I said. "You did it right. They'd only grown."

"Okay," he said. "Good." He held his hands together in front of him and looked at them. "If it snows again I'll do it when I get in. I'm back at seven."

I said that would be kind. He went back out by the side gate and I bolted it after him. It was nearly dark. From the window I could see his half, clear and flat, and the snow starting to settle on it again.

## January

Frank brought the downpipe in the middle of January, which was sooner than I'd expected.

It was a dry day, cold and bright, and he didn't get here till after two, because he'd had to go to two shops for the right grey. The cracked length was the top one, just under the gutter at the front. He put his long ladder up beside the front door and I held the bottom of it while he took the old piece off.

At about half past three the door of 16 opened and Tunde came out with a bag for the bins. He saw us and stopped. Then he put the bag down by our wall and came across.

"Can I hold that?"

"This is Frank," I said. "He planted the hedge."

Frank looked down from the ladder. "You'll be Tunde."

They said hello. I let go, and Tunde took hold of the ladder with both hands, the way he had held mine.

"Feet either side," Frank said, "and lean your weight on it. You're not holding it up. You're holding it down."

Tunde moved his feet and leaned. Frank went up.

I stood on the path with nothing to hold. I have held the bottom of his ladder every November for twelve years, and for twenty-two before that, and he had never once told me how.

The new length went in in a quarter of an hour. Frank came down and said thank you, and Tunde said no problem, and picked up his bag and took it to the bins, and went back in.

Frank had his tea in the kitchen with his coat on, standing at the back window. You can see the whole garden from there: the lawn, the washing line, and at the bottom the hedge, both halves the same height now, more or less, and dark.

"Can I ask you something," I said. "The shirts. When you went out and did them all again, every week."

He didn't say anything.

"I know about the collars," I said. "Was that all it was?"

He looked at his tea for a while. Then he said, "It was the shirts, Ros." He put his cup down.

It was what I'd told Tunde in December about the shoots on his half. I'd meant it, more or less. I didn't know if Frank had, and I could see that I wasn't going to.

After a while I said, "I do them by the side seams now. I have since you went."

"I know," he said. "I've seen."

He went before it got dark. He said the gutters looked all right for another year, and I said I'd see him in November, and he said I would.

Before the post office shut I walked down and bought a pad of squared paper. I wrote on the first sheet.

*Tunde, thank you for holding Frank's ladder. In May, when it needs doing, would you do the top of the hedge with your trimmer, the whole way along, mine as well? I'll hold your ladder. Rosalind, 14.*

I read it twice, and I didn't change anything. Then I put my coat on, and went round, and put it through his door before he left for work.
