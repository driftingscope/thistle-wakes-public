title: "Flitting"

I washed out the press last. The press is the cupboard by the fire, where the plates go and the tea and the good cups, and I always do it last, because it's the one thing in a house that's still yours when everything else is on the cart.

The cart from Balgray had come at seven, with a lad from Balgray and two horses, and by half past eight Dod had the beds and the kist and the dresser roped on, and the chairs legs-up on top like beasts on their backs. Sandy was asleep in the clothes basket in the corner of the kitchen because there was nowhere else left to put him. It was the twenty-eighth of November, and when the cart came it was still dark. Along the road you could hear other carts going, and men shouting to horses you couldn't see. Every farm in the parish was flitting somebody that day, or getting somebody.

I had the shelves done and was doing the inside of the door when I came on Isa's name. She'd written it in pencil near the bottom, where she could reach, the first week we came, at Whitsunday a year and a half ago. *Isa Lindsay*, and under it, *Nether Craigs*.

She was standing behind me with her coat already on. She was nine. Balgray would be her fourth house and her fourth school.

I put the cloth over it and rubbed, and it went. It was never hard to get off. Pencil on paint.

She didn't say anything. She had, once, at the house before this one, when she was seven. She'd asked me why, and I'd said you leave a house the way you'd want to find it, and that was the end of it. I don't think I'd thought about it before I said it. It was what my mother said, about everything. Since then she'd written her name in the press in every house, and I'd washed it out of every press, and neither of us had said.

---

We passed the Duncans' cart where the road goes down to the burn. They were going to Nether Craigs, to our house; Duncan was fee'd as horseman in Dod's place. Dod got down and the two men stood at the horses' heads and talked about the road. Mrs Duncan stayed up on her load. She had a baby inside her coat and a girl beside her about Isa's age, holding a clock on her knees with both arms round it.

"You'll find it clean," I said up to her, because I wanted her to know.

"I'm sure," she said.

The girl looked at Isa and Isa looked at the girl, and nobody said anything, and then the men came back and we went on.

---

Balgray is four miles. We got there at eleven, and the house was empty, and swept, and the grate had been blacked. The floor was still damp in the corners. The family before us had gone out that morning, the same as we had. Whoever she was, she'd known what she was doing.

I opened the press to see the size of it before the dresser came in, and the shelves were still damp, washed that morning like mine. The door had been washed too, all but a strip down the inside of it, not much wider than my hand. You could see the edges where the cloth had stopped, because the paint was cleaner on either side.

The strip was full of names.

They were all in pencil, in different hands, some big and some careful. *Jas. Fyfe, Mart. 1919.* *Nan Ogilvie, Whitsun 1923.* *Will Ogilvie.* *Bella Robb, Martinmas 1927*, with a line under it. There were eleven or twelve. Some of them had been gone over a second time where they'd faded. The oldest were so faint I had to swing the door round to the window to read them.

The last one was *Chrissie Kidd, Whit. 1932*. That would be the girl who'd gone out this morning, if they'd had a girl. I hadn't thought until then that they would have.

She'd washed the whole door round them. She'd have had to go carefully, with the cloth folded small. You don't do that by accident.

I heard Isa come in behind me. She came up to the press and saw what I was looking at, and she read down the names without touching them, the way you read stones in a kirkyard. Then she looked at me.

I gave her the pencil out of my apron, the one I keep for the lists.

She wrote *Isa Lindsay* under Chrissie Kidd, and stopped, and looked at the others with their terms after them, and then at me. I could see she didn't know if she was allowed that part. I took the pencil back off her and wrote *Martinmas 1934* after it, in my own hand, the way the others had it.

Then Dod shouted for somebody to take the end of the dresser, and I went.

I thought about Nether Craigs a good while that day. The Duncan girl would be opening the press about now, with her clock. She'd find it clean.
