title: "After the horn"

*A short story. Made up, like the others.*

The five ewes come out of the letting-out pen at the top of the field and stop, the way every lot has stopped since nine this morning, and look down the hill at the post, where I am standing with my young collie, Moss, pressed against my leg.

From here they are five stones. One is paler than the others.

"When you're ready, Eirlys," says the judge, from his car behind me with the window down.

Ten minutes, the schedule says. When they're gone he sounds the horn.

I send Moss away. He goes out wide on the right, flat and quick, the way he is meant to, and then wider than he is meant to, along the far wall, and then he goes behind the gorse and I can't see him at all. This is the part where you stand there with your hands in your pockets so nobody can see what they're doing. There's a gap in the gorse where he should show again, and for long enough to count to four he doesn't, and I think: he has crossed over, he has cut in, he has found a rabbit, he has gone home.

Then he's there, behind them, exactly where he should be, and he drops without being asked.

The pale ewe turns round to look at him. The other four don't. She puts her head down and stamps once, which at that distance is a small white jolt, and Moss stays down, and I give him the whistle to walk on, and he gets up and walks on, slow, and she looks at him for one more step and then thinks better of it and turns, and all five come off the top together, and this is the lift.

They come straight. They come down the middle of the field like something poured, and for half the fetch there's nothing for me to do but keep my mouth shut. The fetch gates are two hurdles halfway down with a gap between them, and the line is through the gap. At the last moment the pale one leans a fraction to her left, the other four lean with her, and they go round the outside of the left-hand hurdle with a yard to spare.

You can't go back for a gate. I don't say anything. Moss doesn't know; he's watching sheep, not hurdles. That's gone, I think, and then I stop thinking about it, because there's the post to go round.

They have to pass behind me, between me and the judge's car. They come round tight, close enough to hear them breathe, close enough that I can see the pale one isn't pale at all but just a cleaner sheep than the others, as if she'd had a wash. Moss brings them round, and then it's the drive, which is him pushing them away from me, up the field, to the first pair of gates.

He's young, and on the drive the whole of him wants to run round and get in front of them. That's what a dog is for, as far as a dog is concerned. Every few yards I give him the stop whistle, and every time he drops, and every time he looks back at me with one ear up, which I've decided means *are you sure*. Somewhere behind the rope a child asks if the black dog is winning. Somebody drops a flask lid. A collie in the back of a pickup barks twice at nothing and is shushed.

The first drive gates they go through dead centre, all five, and I hear my own breath come out.

The cross-drive is the long line across the face of the field, left to right, and for about forty yards of it everything is right. The sheep are walking, not trotting. Moss is behind them at the distance he should be, flanking a few feet one way, a few feet the other, holding the line without me. It's as good as I've seen him do it. Probably nobody but me and the judge and Glyn, who is waiting by the trailers with his dog, knows that it's good. To everyone else it is some sheep walking across a field.

Then the second gates, a bit low, but through. Then the turn, and they come back towards the pen, and I can leave the post.

The pen is a small square of hurdles with a gate, and a rope on the gate. Once I pick up the rope I can't let go of it until it's finished, and I can't touch a sheep. I stand with the rope in my left hand and my right arm out, and the five of them come up to the mouth of the pen and stop and look in, the way sheep look into a pen, as if it were a cupboard with something in it.

I'm not looking at my watch. I don't need to. I know roughly where we are.

One goes in. Then two together. Then the fourth, which turns round once inside to see what she's done. The pale one stays at the mouth. She looks at the pen. She looks at me. She looks at Moss, who is lying flat with his chin on the grass about four yards off, and she looks back at me.

If I flap at her, she goes, and she goes the wrong way, and the four inside come out after her. So I don't. I give Moss the walk-on, which in his case is a whistle so soft it's mostly breath, and he lifts his body without lifting his head and comes one step. She shifts her weight. I put the arm out a little further. We are all three of us standing in a field in September deciding something.

The horn goes.

She walks into the pen.

It doesn't count, after the horn. I close the gate anyway, because you close the gate. Then I let go of the rope, and say his name, and he comes to my leg as if he'd been there all along.

The letter-out is already sending the next five down from the top. They come out and stop and look down the hill at the post. I walk off along the rope with Moss, and somebody says "Unlucky," and somebody else says "Good drive," and I say thanks to both.
