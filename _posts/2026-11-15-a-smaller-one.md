Guido Rey's book *The Matterhorn* (1907, translated from the Italian by J. E. C. Eaton; it's [on the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/cu31924028501876)) tells the story of the race to climb the mountain in the early 1860s: Edward Whymper against John Tyndall, then against the Italians, with the guide Jean-Antoine Carrel changing sides between them. In the middle of it is a man who carried the tent.

His name was Luc Meynet, and he came from Breuil, the hamlet at the foot of the Italian side. Whymper first took him on in 1862 as a porter. He was on several of the failed attempts after that, and Rey gives him a paragraph of his own:

> The figure of the poor hunchback of Breuil—for so Whymper is wont to call his porter—is one of the most pleasing and most interesting in the whole story. Always ready and willing to start, he was always excellent when at work, and his very deformity enabled him to carry the tent; in the bivouacs he was a useful and cheerful companion.

Rey then retells what Whymper wrote about him: Meynet on the Col du Lion "full of humility and tact, contenting himself with the remains of his 'Monsieur's' meal", grateful for "a poor sleeping-place on the threshold of the tent", and falling to his knees and weeping at the view the first time he saw it. Years later, Rey adds, Meynet reached the summit himself, and "he is said to have exclaimed, on arriving there, that he could hear angels singing and that he could now die happy." Then: "His was a pure and beautiful mountaineer's soul."

It's an affectionate portrait. It's also one in which every line was written by somebody else: Whymper, Rey, the unnamed people who said what he said at the top. In it, Meynet is humble, grateful and moved. He eats what's left and sleeps in the doorway.

## The pipe

Rey's notes at the back of the book have one more story, and this one is introduced differently: "Luc Meynet was wont to relate the following anecdote about his adventures with Whymper." It's the only story in the book that's given as Meynet's own.

The party was resting on the Furggenjoch after an attempt in which "the whole party had all but been swept away by an avalanche of stones". With them was Michel Croz, the guide from Chamonix. Whymper was taking barometer readings. Croz was smoking. Meynet had left his pipe at home, so he waited until Croz had finished, took his hat off, and asked politely to borrow it.

"You might as well have left your head there too, *drôle de bossu*," Croz said. "Do you imagine I am going to lend my pipe to a half-man like you?"

Meynet didn't answer. Whymper had heard, and came over with two cigars: "Here, you smoke too." And then, in the note's words, "Luc proudly lit a cigar, straightened himself to the best of his ability, and strutted about in front of Croz, puffing clouds of smoke into his face."

On the way down the Furggen glacier, the story goes on, Croz walked fast, "hoping to make Luc, who was laden with the tent, slip", and Meynet handled the rope so that the jerks didn't pull him over. Then Croz fell into a crevasse, and Meynet held him.

This isn't the man in the paragraph above. He doesn't eat the leftovers here. He blows smoke in the face of a guide Rey calls "the famous Chamonix guide", out-manages him on the rope with a tent on his back, and saves his life. If Meynet told it the way the note tells it, he told it as a story about himself, and in it he wins.

## Three morals

The story has a moral, and it's given to Whymper, who "turned to Croz and said: 'Sachez, Croz, qu'on a souvent besoin d'un plus petit que soi.'" Know, Croz, that we often need someone smaller than ourselves.

That sentence is La Fontaine's. It's the second line of "The Lion and the Rat" (*Fables*, Book II, 1668), the fable where a lion spares a rat, and the rat later gnaws through the net that has caught him. French schoolchildren still learn it by heart. Whymper, a Londoner, could have known it. So could Meynet, telling it afterwards. Whichever of them said it, the line that closes the story is a borrowed one, and it fits so well that it may have been added in the telling. I can't tell which from Rey.

Then Rey adds a moral of his own: "This tale shows the keen antagonism which existed at that time between Valtournanche guides and those from other places." For him it's a story about local rivalry, the Italian valley against Chamonix.

So one story ends three ways. In Rey's, it's about guides. In Whymper's (or La Fontaine's), it's about the use of small people. The third ending is the one before the moral: Croz in a crevasse, and the man he called half a man holding the rope.

Rey gives only one occasion when Whymper, Croz and Meynet were on the mountain together, and it fits the story: an attempt from the Breuil side in June 1865, stopped by falling stones, after which Croz "had an engagement at Chamonix" and left. A little over three weeks later, on 14 July, Croz reached the summit with Whymper from the Swiss side, and died on the way down when the man behind him slipped. Rey's note doesn't say when Meynet told the story, or to whom. It only says that he used to tell it.

## A note, after reading Whymper

After this went up I read Whymper's own *Scrambles Amongst the Alps*, which I hadn't when I wrote it. Rey's paragraph is fair to it in one way: Whymper calls Meynet "the hunchback" again and again, and the leftovers, the place at the door of the tent and the kneeling on the Col are all there. But Rey kept the humble half and left out much of the rest.

Whymper first sees Meynet coming home with a mule, picking up his brother's children and putting them in the empty panniers, and then hears the party come on "carolling, as if this was not a world of woe". He writes that "few peasants are more agreeable companions, or better climbers". On the difficult parts Meynet "was always merriest", and on the most difficult he kept saying "We can only die once". In 1863, when Whymper and Carrel disagreed about a route, Meynet sided with Carrel, and Carrel's route was taken. Twice he put "important cheese-making operations" before the mountain.

And on 21 June 1865, the attempt the pipe story seems to belong to, Whymper says the falling stones sent all four men dashing for cover in a panic that was "indescribably ludicrous" (he hid in a cleft himself). When he then climbed on to see whether the rocks above would go, "the little hunchback alone was closely following me—with a broad grin upon his face, and the tent upon his shoulder". Almer sat with his face in his hands. Croz shouted "Come down, come down". The book that compares Meynet to a beaten dog also has him grinning while the others stay behind. That's much closer to the man in the pipe story.

One thing fits less well than I said above. In Rey's note, the party rests on the Furggenjoch and goes down "the Furggen glacier" to Breuil. In Whymper's account of 21 June they reach the Breuiljoch, find it cut off from the Furggengletscher by a wall of rock, and go back to Breil. The stones match. The places don't quite, and I don't know the ground well enough to say whether the two names could cover the same descent. Either the story belongs to a day Whymper didn't write up, or it moved a little in the telling.
