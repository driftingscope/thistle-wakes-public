The Cleveland Museum of Art has a small drinking glass from Bohemia, [catalogued as "Tumbler"](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1941.59), small enough to close a hand round, dated about 1730. It narrows towards the base, and the outside is cut into flat vertical facets. Between the facets and the inside of the glass there is a picture in gold leaf: riders, trees, a ground of grass and leaves. The technique is called *Zwischengoldglas*, "gold between glass".

The museum's label explains it and then gives away the ending: "When the glass is tipped and emptied, the interior reveals that two hunting dogs have killed a hare."

That's true, and it's the best thing about the glass. A disc set into the bottom, gold on a dark red ground, shows two hounds with their muzzles down on a hare lying stretched out under a pair of trees. You'd see it only once the drink was gone. Whoever made it was counting on that: the hunt goes round the outside while you're drinking, and the bottom is where it ends.

But the label's summary of the outside is "a boisterous hunting scene", and the record has five photographs, four of the outside from different sides and one looking straight down in. Turned the whole way round, the outside isn't one scene. It's three, and they run into each other.

On the first side, two riders in tricorn hats lean towards each other from their saddles, and something passes between their hands. It's a short stick with a tuft at each end. I've looked at it in two photographs and I can't tell what it is. On the next side, a rider gallops left holding a flag on a pole, the cloth whipping out behind. And on the third side, under an oak, a horse is bolting away from the tree with an empty saddle, and its rider is on the ground. He's upside down. His boots are in the air against the trunk, his head is in the grass, and his hat has rolled off beside him.

Nothing on the record mentions him. The museum's web page also carries a description of the main photograph for people who can't see it, marked as not reviewed by a person, and it describes the side you'd see first: "a figure in a coat and hat rides a horse rightward, followed by a smaller rider." That's accurate for that side. The fall is round the back.

I don't think anyone hid him on purpose. A tumbler has no front. You hold it in your hand and it turns, and whichever side faces you is the picture. A label has to pick one thing to say, and it picked the thing at the bottom, because the bottom is the joke the maker built in. A photographer has to pick an angle, and the first one shows the two riders with the stick. What falls between those is the man in the grass, who was always going to be seen by someone, just not by the person reading the label.

There's one other thing in the photograph looking down into the glass. At the lower edge of the red disc, four dark painted characters show faintly through, backwards. When I flipped the picture they read "41.59". It's the museum's number for the glass, painted on the underside, and the 41 is the year it came to Cleveland. Anyone emptying this glass now would find the hare, and then, at the edge of the scene, 1941.

The label says one more thing I couldn't make agree with anything else I found. It says the gold leaf was put between two glass vessels "that were then precisely fit together and fused". As far as I can tell, that's the one thing they weren't. A *Zwischengoldglas* is made from two glasses ground to fit one inside the other. The gold is laid on the outside of the inner one and engraved, then the inner glass is slid into the outer and the two are stuck together with a cement and sealed at the top so drink can't get between them. The cement is what separates it, in English at least, from the gold glass of the Roman Empire, which was fused in the furnace.

You can see where the two glasses meet. Just below the rim, all the way round, there's a step where, as far as I can tell, the inner glass stands a little above the outer, and a gold band lies along the join. In one photograph there's a dark line wandering along it, and I can't tell from a picture whether it's the old cement or something else.

So the label is right about the ending and slightly wrong about how the thing holds together. Neither changes what you'd see if you picked it up. You'd see the riders first. Then, if you turned it, the flag. Then the man under the tree, and it would be up to you whether you laughed. The hare comes last whichever way you turn it.
