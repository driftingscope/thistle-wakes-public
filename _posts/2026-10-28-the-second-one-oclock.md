title: "The second one o'clock"
date: 2026-10-28 13:08:00
---

I was born first, at eight minutes to two in the morning, and my sister was born twenty-two minutes after me. On my birth certificate the time is 1.52 a.m. On Nell's it's 1.14 a.m.

You only get a time on your certificate in England if you're a twin. Our mother says that was the first thing the government ever did for us, and it got it wrong.

It was the night the clocks went back, in October 1986. Mum told it the same way every time. A porter came round the ward at two o'clock with a stepladder to put the clocks back an hour, because that was his job and nobody had told him to wait, and the midwife looked up at the wall when Nell came out and wrote down what it said. "I did that hour twice," Mum said, "and I'd not wish it on anyone."

So I'm the elder, and on paper I'm not. Mum kept both certificates in a biscuit tin with the TV licence, and Nell found them when we were nine. She took them to Dad in the kitchen and stood there holding them up until he stopped what he was doing.

Dad thought about it for a while. Then he said the first one o'clock was mine, because I was there for it and Nell wasn't. And the second one o'clock, the one that only comes round once a year, when the clock goes back to one and has to get to two all over again, was Nell's. For that hour, she was the eldest.

He meant it as a way to stop us arguing, and it didn't, but it did become a thing we did. Every year on the night the clocks went back, we stayed up. Dad put the kitchen clock back at two, and for the next hour Nell gave orders and I followed them.

Most of what she ordered was rubbish. When we were ten, she ordered me to call her Your Eldership, and I did, for an hour. At twelve she had the top bunk, which was the only thing she ever kept. At seventeen she ordered me to ring Gavin Tierney and tell him she didn't fancy him, at quarter past one in the morning, and I did that as well. Gavin Tierney's mum answered.

The rest of the year, I was the eldest, and I knew what that was. I'm the one who rang the council about Mum's bins. I'm the one who lives two streets from Nell and has her spare key, and has had her two kids on Tuesday afternoons since the older one started school. When something needed deciding for both of us, I decided it, and what I decided, mostly, was to stay where I was.

This year our fortieth was on the Monday and the clocks went back on the Saturday night, so Nell came to stay at mine and Dan had the kids. We had a takeaway. We watched something neither of us was watching. My phone was on the arm of the settee, face down, and on it was an email from the university in St John's, Newfoundland, offering me the three-year post I'd applied for in June. I hadn't told anyone I'd applied, because I hadn't thought I'd get it. It asked for my answer by Monday.

I'd had it a fortnight. I hadn't told Nell. It wasn't that I didn't know what to say. I knew exactly what the eldest would say, because I'd been her for forty years. She'd say stay.

The first one o'clock went past. Nell talked about Dan's mother. I said things back. At eight minutes to two she said, "Happy birthday, technically," which she says every year whatever the date, and I said it back, which I also do every year.

Our phones don't wait for a porter any more. At 01:59 they both said 01:59, and then they both said 01:00.

"Right," Nell said, and put her feet on the coffee table, which she's allowed to do in that hour.

I turned the phone over and gave it to her.

She read it twice, scrolling up and down. Then she put it on her knee and looked at me for long enough that I started explaining. The post was three years. Ocean science. It was the thing I'd done my doctorate on and then not done since, because the job here was here. I said she'd need to sort out Tuesdays. I said the younger one was only seven. I said I'd already decided, really, and I just wanted her to know.

"You've not decided," Nell said. "You've waited till one o'clock."

I didn't say anything to that, because it was true.

"Fine." She sat up straight, the way she did when we were ten. "First order. You're going."

"Nell."

"Second order. You don't ring me on Tuesdays to ask how Tuesday went. Dan's mum can have them. She keeps asking."

"She lets them have crisps for tea."

"Then they'll have crisps for tea." She held up the phone. "Third order. How far behind is it?"

"Three and a half hours," I said. "Two and a half this week, because theirs don't go back till next Sunday."

"I didn't ask about this week." She was trying not to laugh, and not managing. "So you'll be three and a half hours behind me. Which means I'm always three and a half hours further on. So then I'll be the eldest. All day, every day. That's my third order."

"That's not an order, that's just a fact you've made up."

"It's my hour."

She gave me my phone back and I wrote the reply while she watched. She didn't say anything while I was typing, which is the longest she has ever not said anything.

At ten to two she went up to bed. She said she wasn't staying up to the end of it, because the end of it was the bit where I was in charge again and she couldn't be doing with that.

I sat for a bit after she'd gone. Then I looked at the email in my sent folder. It says 01:47. It doesn't say which one.
