This is my third essay about Félix Guicherd's drawings of silks in Cleveland, and I expected it to be the last and the smallest. In [Before 1962]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/08/before-1962.html) I looked at his drawing of an eagle silk, 1962.264, signed "Juillet – août 1948". The museum has a second drawing of the same silk, 1964.442, given by the same dealer in the same year. I'd only glanced at it.

It comes in two parts. Part a is a full sheet, signed in pencil "Guicherd, septembre 1948", the month after the first. Part b is a strip of the same paper, about seventeen centimetres high.

![Left: a sheet of squared paper on which the top of a double-headed eagle, a small human face on its chest and a few letters of script have been drawn square by square, left white against a grey wash. Right: a long strip of squared paper with a line of angular script drawn in black between the heads of two eagles, with a small arrow and a pencil word at the top centre]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/wrong-side-two-sheets.jpg)

*Cleveland 1964.442.a (left) and 1964.442.b (right). Cleveland Museum of Art, CC0.*

Both are on the same printed paper as the first drawing, from P. Cuilleron of Lyon, marked "8 en 10. N° 2". Both are the same silk: double-headed eagles, a figure on each eagle's chest, and a line of Kufic script along the top. But they're drawn differently. On the strip, the pattern is filled in black on the bare grid, dark on light, as the silk looks in the museum's photograph. On the sheet it's the other way round: the eagle and the letters are left white, and the ground around them is washed grey.

That could just be two ways of drawing. Then I looked at the letters.

## The letters

Many of the letters in this inscription are tall strokes with a little flag at the top, and on the silk the flags point left. On the strip they point left too. On the sheet they point right.

![Three strips of squared paper, one above the other. Top: part of a line of angular script in black on a light grid, ending at an eagle's head on the right. Middle: similar letters in white on a grey-washed grid, with their flags pointing the other way and printed text running up the right margin. Bottom: the middle image flipped left to right, so that the printed text in the margin now reads backwards and the letters match the top strip]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/wrong-side-letters.jpg)

*Top: the end of the inscription on 1964.442.b, next to the right-hand eagle. Middle: the letters on 1964.442.a, as drawn. Bottom: the same, flipped left to right.*

Flip the sheet left to right and its letters line up with the end of the line on the strip: the same flagged strokes, the same pair of tall uprights, the same low run of box-shaped letters ending against the eagle. The scan itself isn't reversed. Cuilleron's name, printed up the margin, reads the right way round, and in the flipped version it's backwards.

So the sheet shows the silk in mirror image, and in negative: light where the silk is dark, dark where it's light. That's roughly what you'd see if you turned the cloth over. And in the bottom corner, under Guicherd's pencil, someone has written in ink, in English:

![Handwriting below the edge of a squared grid: in pencil, "Relevé des motifs supérieurs", a signature "Guicherd" and "septembre 1948"; to the left, in darker ink, a short word followed by a colon and "wrong side up"]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/wrong-side-corner.jpg)

*Bottom right corner of 1964.442.a, from the museum's full-size scan.*

Guicherd's pencil says "Relevé des motifs supérieurs", a record of the upper motifs. The ink says "wrong side up", after a short word with a colon that I can't read for certain. It might be "dsg", for design. In English, the wrong side of a cloth is its back. I think whoever wrote it had noticed the same thing, and that the sheet is a drawing of the back of the silk.

I don't know why he drew it that way, or who wrote the note, or when. Neither record mentions it. Both parts have the same title, "Schematic Drawing of Buyid Silk (1962.264)", and the same date, "1900s".

## Why it took the letters

What surprised me is how well the mirror hides. The eagles are symmetrical: two heads turned outwards, two wings, a griffin in each foot. Turn one over and you get the same eagle. The figure on its chest faces straight out. Even the sheet's place on the silk doesn't give it away, because the inscription runs between two eagles, so letters to the right of an eagle are just as likely to be letters to the left of the next one, reversed.

In the pattern, only the writing goes one way. I'd looked at the first drawing, which has no letters on it, and written that the eagle was drawn "light against a dark ground" as if that were simply how this kind of drawing is done. The eagles can't tell you which way round it is. But Guicherd drew more than the eagles. He drew the outline of the cloth too, where it's torn away, and only one place on the silk is torn like that.

![Three tall images side by side. Left: a drawing on squared paper of a double-headed eagle, light on a black ground, with the black ending in a ragged edge down the left side and a deep bay cut into it at the bottom left, below which the beak of another eagle appears. Middle: the same drawing flipped left to right, so the ragged edge and the bay are on the right. Right: a photograph of the brown silk, cropped to one eagle, whose cloth ends on the right in the same ragged edge and bay, with the same beak below]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/wrong-side-torn-edge.jpg)

*Left: 1964.441 as drawn. Middle: the same, flipped left to right. Right: the right-hand eagle in the middle row of the silk 1962.264, cropped and scaled by me to match.*

As drawn, the cloth ends to the left of the eagle, with a bay torn out below it. Nowhere on the silk looks like that. The left-hand side of the silk runs nearly straight down past all three left-hand eagles, with no bay. Flip the drawing and it matches the middle eagle in the right-hand column. The cloth ends just beyond its wing, then cuts in under its tail, and below that only part of the next eagle's head is left, beak and all. I laid one over the other, and the edge follows the edge most of the way down.

So the first drawing is the back as well, light on dark and reversed, just like the second. Of the three pieces of paper, only the strip shows the silk the way the museum photographs it.

The strip has one more note. At the top, in the middle, Guicherd has drawn an arrow and written "pointe".

![The word "pointe" in pencil above a short arrow pointing down at a line of the grid]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/wrong-side-pointe.jpg)

*Top centre of 1964.442.b.*

As far as I can tell, in Lyon weaving "à pointe" is the term for setting up a loom for a symmetrical design, one that repeats in mirror image about an axis. The arrow sits midway between the two eagles, which are mirror images of each other. But the inscription runs straight across under the arrow without reversing. Whatever mirrors the eagles doesn't mirror the words.
