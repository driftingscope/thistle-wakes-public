The Cleveland Museum of Art has two copies of [a small French bronze medal](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1921.486), each 5.1 centimetres across, both given by Lawrence Hitchcock in 1921. On the front are two men in profile, one just behind the other, so their faces nearly overlap. The one at the back wears a wide hat with the brim turned up, from the seventeenth century. The one in front has a beard and the round steel helmet the French army started issuing in 1915. Behind them, small and to the left, is a cathedral with a single spire. Above them is the word SEMPER, Latin for "always," and beside them two dates, one over the other: 1674, 1916.

On the back is a portrait of Turenne, the marshal who commanded Louis XIV's armies on the Rhine, with long curled hair and a sash across his armour. Around his head is a line attributed to him: *Il ne faut pas qu'il y ait un homme d'armes au repos tant qu'un Allemand restera de ce côté du Rhin.* There shouldn't be a soldier at rest while one German is still on this side of the Rhine. The words are set in short lines on either side of him, and his hair is in the way of every one, so you read *qu'il y*, then jump across his face to *ait un*. *Homme d',* across, *armes au*. You can't get through the sentence without looking at him five times.

He's supposed to have said it in the winter of 1674, during the campaign that drove the imperial armies out of Alsace. One version has him saying it to an officer just before the battle of Turckheim in January 1675. The wording changes from book to book, and I couldn't find it written down anywhere close to his own time. By 1916 that hardly mattered. Germany had taken Alsace in 1871, after the Franco-Prussian War, and the line suited that grievance. This medal quotes it again. The man from 1674 and the man from 1916 are drawn as one shape. SEMPER says it's the same war, and always has been.

The cathedral is Strasbourg's, which was built with only one of its two planned spires. And Strasbourg wasn't French in 1674. It was a free city of the Holy Roman Empire, with its own bridge over the Rhine, and imperial armies used that bridge to get into Alsace more than once during that war. France took the city in 1681, seven years after the first date on the medal. In 1916, the second date, it had been in Germany for forty-five years. It's on the west bank, so by the letter of Turenne's line, the Germans it names were already on this side of the Rhine, in the city on the medal.

So under the word SEMPER, "always", the medal puts a cathedral behind its two soldiers, and that cathedral was in France in neither of the years printed beside it. I don't think that was a mistake. Whoever designed it knew exactly where Strasbourg was in 1916. Putting it there was the point: this is what the man in the helmet is for, and the man in the hat is there to say it's been his job before.

The signature on the back reads *I. P. Boulongne*, with an n. The museum spells his name *Boulongue*, with a u, as Louis E. Paul Boulongue, and says he was active from 1908 to 1914, which leaves 1916 out, and it dates the medal "c. 1908–1916" even though 1916 is cast into the front. I don't know anything else about him.

When Hitchcock gave Cleveland the two medals, Strasbourg had been French again since the end of 1918. In 1921, for the first time since the medal was cast, the cathedral on it was in the country the medal said it belonged to. It stayed that way until 1940.
