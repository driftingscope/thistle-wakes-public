The front of [this plate](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1969.254) is the reason anyone kept it. It's Delft, tin-glazed earthenware painted in cobalt blue, 35.6 centimetres across, with a thin ochre line round the rim. In the middle is a squat, ribbed vase holding a fan of long feathers, spread like a peacock's tail behind a few round flowers and two bunches of berries. Six big lobed flower heads go round the border, with small vases between them. The pattern is common enough that dealers have a name for it, the peacock plate, and the Victoria and Albert Museum has [one very like it](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O89100/dish-de-klaauw-factory), yellow rim and all, dated 1770 to 1800.

Cleveland's record is short: "Plate, 1700s. Probably by De Porceleyne Claeuw Factory." Then the size, and the man who gave it in 1969, George H. Worthington, who also gave the museum the [enamel with the question mark in its title](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1969.256). The museum photographed the back as well, and the back is where most of the plate's writing is.

In the centre, painted in the same blue as the front, is a small mark: a long stroke ending in a splay of short ones, like a bird's foot, with a little hook beside it. I take that to be the claw, *de claeuw*, and the reason the record can name a factory at all, even with a "probably". In 1764, after a potter named Justus Brouwer complained that other factories were putting his mark on their wares, the Delft city council made all twenty-three potteries register their marks, and copying one became punishable by a fine. The claw on the V&A's dish has the number 13 painted beside it. The hook on this one could be a number too. I can't read it.

Off to one side, in red paint, is "69.254", the number the museum gave it in 1969. Near that is an older paper label with a red border: "C.M.A. 886.20". The record lists a former number, 886.1920. If the 1920 is a year, which is how it looks, the plate had a number at this museum forty-nine years before it was given. The record doesn't say how it came to be there in the meantime, and I'm not going to guess.

Then there's the cross. A grey band runs straight across the back from rim to rim, and another crosses it at a right angle, exactly through the middle. It isn't part of the decoration, and it isn't a break: the blue claw runs through it unbroken, and the front shows nothing. Where each arm reaches the rim, there's a small rectangle outlined in fine scratches, as if something flat and metal sat there, and on the front, at the same four places, the rim is chipped through the white glaze to the clay underneath. A short crack runs in from the rim at the top one.

I think that's a hanger. Four clips gripped the rim, four straps ran inward from them and met in the middle, and over a long time on a wall they left their shape in grime on the glaze. I don't know whose wall. It could have been a house, or the museum's own gallery. I haven't found anyone describing this plate, so that's my reading of the photographs and nothing firmer. It does account for everything I can see.

So the back of the plate has been written on three times. The maker put the claw there, because the city required it, so you'd know who made it. The museum put its numbers there, twice, so it would know which plate this was. Both were put there to say what the plate is. The cross is the only mark nobody meant to make, and the only one that says what happened to it: someone held it up by its edges, front out, for long enough to leave a mark. The hanger ran right over the claw.
