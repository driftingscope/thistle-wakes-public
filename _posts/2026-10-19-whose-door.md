[This doorway](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1959.342) is the whole front entrance of a house in Painesville, Ohio, and it is nearly three metres tall. It has a plain panelled door and a pair of fluted columns on each side. Above it there's a cornice carved with acanthus leaves and draped swags, and narrow leaded windows run up both sides and across the top. The Cleveland Museum of Art dates it to 1821 and credits Jonathan Goldsmith, a builder who came to northeastern Ohio from Connecticut around 1802 and put up dozens of houses there. This one was Isaac Gillet's. I read that the house has since been pulled down, but I couldn't confirm it or find when.

In the museum it stands in a gallery, flat against a wall painted dark blue. Through the side windows, where a hall would have been, you see the wall.

There's a brass plate on the door at eye height, with a scallop shell at the top and "I. GILLET" engraved across the middle. I took it for a nameplate. It's a knocker. The frame around the name, two turned posts with a bar across the bottom, must be the part that swings. So the one fitting on that door meant for someone who hasn't been let in yet does two things: it tells you whose house you've come to, and it lets you ask to be let in.

The knocker has its own record, and it didn't come in with the door. Mrs A. Dean Perry gave the museum the doorway in 1959, along with a mantel and some mouldings from the same house. The knocker came in five years later, in 1964, given by someone else, Philip A. Child. The record doesn't say how he came to have it. The knocker went back on the door, and both records now give the same gallery.

It also isn't as old as the door. The museum dates it to about 1840, nineteen years after the doorway. Under the bottom bar, half hidden by it, a maker's name is stamped into the brass: "LEWIS, WALL ST. N.Y." So at some point, probably after the door had been in use for years, someone ordered a knocker with his name on it from a shop in New York and had it fitted. What the gallery shows is the front of his house as it looked around 1840, not as Goldsmith built it.

The records seem to have taken it that way too. Both of them credit Goldsmith and the Lewis firm together, the doorway as well as the knocker. And the knocker's description isn't about a knocker. It's the doorway's description, word for word: "This elaborate doorway originally came from a house designed and built by Jonathan Goldsmith…" It may only be a copy-and-paste. But it isn't wrong, exactly. A knocker on its own is a small brass fitting, about fifteen centimetres high. What it's for only makes sense on a door.

The mantel and mouldings are listed with no gallery and no photograph. The door is the part of the house that went on display, and the knocker is the part of the door that faces out. It can't do the second of its jobs any more, since there's no inside to be let into. It still does the first. Everyone who walks through that gallery is told whose door it is.
