title: "The present plan"

The Met has a drawing by the Bolognese painter and architect Flaminio Innocenzo Minozzi (1735–1817) catalogued as [*Description of a Plan with Three Figures and Divan Below*](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/388224). It's a big sheet, 49 by 38 centimetres, and most of it is handwriting. Across the top, in a careful, looping italic hand, is a heading: *Dichiarazione della presente Pianta*, an explanation of the present plan. Below that are about thirty lines of Italian. Below those, inside the same ruled frame and cut off from the writing by a single line, is a sofa.

There's no plan on the sheet. The writing explains one, at length, and the plan isn't there.

I read the text from the Met's full-size photograph. The Italian is eighteenth-century and loosely spelled ("dimetro", "Magazeni"), and my translations below are rough, but the building comes through clearly enough to walk around in.

The first sentence apologises. A plan, it says, is the most interesting part of an architectural design, but here only half of it is shown, because the two halves mirror each other perfectly. If the whole plan were shown, you would first see a great semicircle, ringed with warehouses and porticoes, which the writer calls the outer courtyard. You come in by three entrances, "suitably wide and magnificent". In the middle of the straight side is a vestibule, and through it a kind of *tempietto*, a little round temple, and then a second atrium, which the writer compares to the *tablinum* of the ancients, the room in a Roman house where the family's records were kept. By the same analogy, the text says, you could hang portraits here of the princes or private citizens who had distinguished themselves in "protecting, favouring and helping" commerce. On top of the rotunda, in the elevations, there's a statue of Mercury: the round temple is a Pantheon "dedicated to the tutelary deities of Commerce".

So it's a merchants' building, a kind of exchange. There are ground-floor rooms for "various necessary departments" and upstairs apartments for the officials and custodians. There are two inner courtyards, described as "two octagons, or rather two squares with the corners cut off". I like that correction. The sheet looks like a fair copy, neatly lettered with nothing crossed out, so the correction is deliberate: octagon was the easy word, and not quite the true one.

The biggest covered room, the text goes on, "could be called a Basilica for its use", although the ancients made their basilicas rectangular, not round, and "we will now call it, and I do call it, the Forum of the Merchants". Judges sit there. Lawyers, clients, witnesses and notaries come to it. Then comes the sentence the whole design seems to turn on. All those people deal in written deeds, the *carte instrumentate*, "which all too often are prey to the flames". So the archive goes in the safest place in the building: up in the dome, in a walkway that runs "round and round" the drum of the inner dome, isolated and separated from the living quarters, close at hand and far from fire. The inner dome has a large hole in the middle and smaller ones at the sides, and gets its light through big windows in the drum of an outer dome, "which serves as a lid to this mass". The outer dome stands on a larger rotunda, which makes a second walkway on the ground floor, with stairs straight up to the archive.

Read that way, the papers are the heart of the building. The courtyards, the porticoes, the temple to Mercury and the portraits of benefactors are around the outside. At the centre, up in the dome and away from where anyone lives, are the deeds.

Near the end the text lists the drawings that go with it: after the plan, a section across the width of the site, then the elevation of the whole building, facing the warehouses and the semicircular porticoes, and last a section through the round temple. That makes four drawings, and none of them is on this sheet. I searched the Met's collection for Minozzi. Besides this sheet there are eight records under his name: ceilings, altars, a fountain, an urn, a catafalque. None of them is the merchants' forum. The explanation is here and the building isn't, at least not here.

The last sentence is a modesty formula: *Così fosse ideato il Soggetto, come egli fu magistralmente proposto*. Roughly: if only the subject had been designed as masterfully as it was set. "Set" is my guess at *proposto*, and if it's right, someone else chose the subject and the writer was answering it. Minozzi won architecture prizes at Bologna's Accademia Clementina as a young man, starting at fifteen, according to Italian Wikipedia. I couldn't find anything connecting this sheet to one of those competitions, so I'll leave it as a phrase that sounds like an answer to a set question, and no more than that.

Then, below the line, the sofa.

It's long, on a row of tapered legs, with garlands swagged between them, a lattice along the top rail, panels of small flowers, and in the middle of the back a carved crest of flowers with what look like two small birds. It stands on a tiled floor drawn in perspective with faint ruled lines. Coming in from the left are three men in classical dress, cloaks over one shoulder, bare arms. One stands upright with his arm stretched out towards it. One leans over the arm with his hands down on the seat, and the third bends in close behind him to see. They look like they've been sent from the round temple upstairs to inspect the furniture.

I don't know why the sofa is there. Nothing in the text mentions furniture, and the sofa isn't in any of the rooms the text describes. The writing is about a round temple to the gods of commerce and a Roman basilica, a public building for judges and notaries; the sofa belongs in somebody's drawing room. The museum's title gives it the same billing the sheet does, *with Three Figures and Divan Below*, and the record I could read doesn't say how the two came to share a frame. The ruled border runs unbroken around both, so they're one sheet and one frame, and not two pieces pasted together, as far as I can see in the photograph.

My best guess, and it's only a guess, is that the explanation got its sheet first and the bottom quarter stayed blank, because the writer finished sooner than the frame did, and that later someone, perhaps Minozzi, perhaps not, used the good empty paper for something else. Paper cost money. A blank quarter of a big sheet, already framed, would be hard to waste.

But the guess isn't really the point. What I keep coming back to is how the title works. "Description of a Plan" is a fair translation of the heading, and it's the first thing you read in the museum's record. But a description of a plan is normally read with the plan beside it. Here the plan is missing, along with the section, the elevation and the other section, and what's here is the part that was there to support them: the words that explain the pictures. Because the writer was trying to make the drawings clear, the words now do the job alone. You can still find the archive in the dome, the portraits in the second atrium, Mercury on the roof. You can't see any of it.

And under all that, the one thing actually drawn is a sofa that nothing on the sheet explains, with three men in togas testing the cushions.
