The Metropolitan Museum's record is called ["Silhouette of Pearley Rose, to left"](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826472). It's a square of paper about ten centimetres across, dated about 1824, "probably by William Chamberlain". The medium line says "Hollow cut paper". Nothing else in the record describes her.

A hollow-cut is made the opposite way from the silhouette most people picture. You don't cut a black head out and stick it on white. You take a pale sheet and cut the profile out of the middle of it, so the head falls away and leaves a hole the shape of the sitter. Then you put something dark behind the hole. The portrait you're looking at is the dark thing showing through. In the photograph of Pearley Rose, you can see what the dark thing is. It isn't paper. It's a ribbed black cloth, the kind a ribbon is made of, and it sticks out past the edges of the sheet, frayed. Along the top edge there's a row of small white stitches holding the two together. Someone sewed her on.

So the only piece of paper cut exactly to her measure, the one that was her nose and chin and the knot of her hair, is the piece that dropped out. I don't know what happened to it. The record doesn't say, and I don't think anyone noted it. What survives is everything around her.

The second thing you notice, once you look closely, is that she's drawn as well as cut. Scissors can do a nose. They can't do lace. Over the top of her head and down at the back of her neck there's a soft grey frill in pencil, drawn on the paper just outside the hole: the edge of a cap and a ruffled collar, sketched where the paper was still there to take it. The cut gives the shape of the person. The pencil gives the clothes, and it gives them on the part of the sheet that isn't her.

The Met has seven records under Chamberlain's name, all hollow-cuts, all about 1824, and all "probably". He never signed or stamped his work. The reason anyone can attribute anything to him at all is a group of eighty-nine silhouettes his granddaughter gave to the American Antiquarian Society in 1916, cut during a two-year tour through New England in the 1820s. Other sources give his dates as 1790 to 1860, born in Loudon, New Hampshire, buried in Boscawen. Everything else with his name on it, the Met's seven included, is attributed by looking like those.

Six of the seven came to the museum in 1938, in the bequest of a collector called Mary Martin, and their accession numbers run in a row. Abigail Taintor is 38.145.381 (the only one with no photograph). Pearley Rose is .382. [Harriet Case](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826473) is .383, then [Aaron Crane](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826474), then ["young woman, to right"](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826475), then [Giles H. Case](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826476). Someone had written numbers in the top right corners, too: Pearley Rose's sheet says 109 and Harriet Case's says 110. They look like page or item numbers from wherever the sheets were kept together before.

Seen side by side, they're plainly the work of one hand. Each has the same pale grey pencil frill at the cap and collar. Aaron Crane's is the exception: his ruffle and the outline of his hair are in brown ink, more deliberately, as if this one got more time. Harriet Case's sheet has the same ribbed cloth behind it, but her stitches are gone. There's only a line of small holes along the top where they were.

Pearley Rose's name is on her sheet twice. At the top, in a plain hand, in violet ink: "Pearley Rose." At the bottom, much fainter, in pencil, with a looping capital P: the same name again. Harriet Case's sheet also has her name in violet at the top. Violet inks belong mostly to the second half of the nineteenth century, after synthetic dyes came in. I can't test the ink from a photograph, so this is a guess, but if it's one of those, the name at the top was written decades after the cutting, by someone copying the pencil underneath so it could be read.

That makes sense of a thing that otherwise seems odd. Nobody needed a name on the day. The cutter knew who was sitting in front of him, and so did she. The name is for later: for whoever has the sheet once nobody in the room is left, and then, when the pencil fades, for whoever comes after them.

Not all of them kept their names. ["Young woman, to right"](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826475) has the cap, the collar and the cloth, and no writing at all that I can see. Giles H. Case's sheet has nothing on its front but a small pencilled x in one corner, so his name must have come from somewhere else, the back or an old list. And there's a seventh, from a different bequest in 1950, catalogued as ["an unknown young woman"](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/826478). Her sheet is badly foxed and cracked across the top, and the cloth behind her has gone brown. She's wearing a tall comb in her hair, cut with real care, and a frilled collar drawn in grey.

I keep coming back to the shape of the whole thing. A man travelled through New England cutting people's profiles out of paper. The part that was the person fell away. What was kept was a hole with a frill drawn round it, sewn to a piece of black ribbon, with a name added later so the hole would still say who it was. And it works. Two hundred years on, at ten centimetres across, you can see that Pearley Rose had a small straight nose, a slightly receding chin and her hair pinned up high at the back. I know almost nothing else about her. But that much is true, and it was got by cutting carefully around her.
