In the museum's main photograph, [this covered vase](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1969.257) is almost too busy to take in. It's Dutch delftware from the middle of the 1700s, about 25 centimetres tall with its lid on, eight-sided and swelling at the shoulder. On the front, inside a heart-shaped frame, two roosters face each other on a strip of ground, under a sky washed in blue, with a tree leaning in from the right. Flowers moulded in relief, white with red centres and rings of red dots, sit above and below the scene and again on the lid. Dark blue scrolls run down the corners. The lid ends in a fan of leaves, each picked out in blue.

The museum also photographed the vase and the lid apart, and in those pictures they've been turned round. From behind, the vase is plain white, with one green sprig painted up the side: a thin brown stem and a few pairs of leaves. The lid has a smaller sprig of its own. The fan of leaves on top has almost no blue on its back. Whoever painted it did the side that would show, and stopped.

So it was made to stand with its back to something. Delft potteries often sold vases like this in sets of three, five or seven, to stand in a row along the top of a tall cupboard, a *kast*, or on a chimneypiece. I don't know that this one was part of a set. The record doesn't say, and I didn't find a match for it in Cleveland's collection. But the painting knows where the wall was.

The damage is on the back too. Near one corner of the neck, a crack runs down from the rim, and a flake has come away beside it, leaving a hole you can see into. Around the rims of the lid and the foot, the white glaze has chipped off in small pieces, and the clay underneath shows brown. Only the chips on the rims show in the main photograph; the crack doesn't. Turned the way it was meant to face, the vase still looks whole.

The base carries a small axe, painted in dark brown, and the record says the vase was "probably" made at a pottery called Het Bijltje, the Little Axe. I've written before about [a plate from the same donor]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/10/18/held-at-four-points.html) whose back says more than its front. This isn't that. On this vase the back says very little, and the front is where I keep looking.

Look at the roosters closely. Each is drawn in a brown outline, with its wing feathers filled in red and blue. But there are two more patches of the same red and blue that belong to neither bird. One sits by the left rooster's legs, shaped like a tail with nothing attached to it. The other hangs from the right rooster's breast. Work in the Delft potteries was split between specialists, and many hands went into one piece, so my guess is that the colours went on in a separate pass, perhaps by someone else, and a couple of them didn't land where the outline expected. I can't confirm that, and they may be something I'm not reading right.

Whatever they are, nobody painted over them, and there was no turning them to the wall. The back was left plain because no one would see it. These two patches are in the middle of the front, where anyone who looked at the vase at all would see them.
