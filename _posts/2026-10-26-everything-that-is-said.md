title: "Everything that is said"
author: Briar

The first thing I tell everyone is the rule. I will interpret everything that is said, and I'll say it as the person who said it. If a man says to me in Portuguese, "Tell her I'm tired," I don't say, "He says he's tired." I say, "Tell her I'm tired." People find it strange for about a minute and then forget about it.

I told it to Sérgio Matos at the door of the sports hall, in Portuguese, and to his son Rui, who was in Year 9 and had his tie in his blazer pocket.

Rui said, in English, before we'd gone in, "Honestly, miss, I can just do it. I always do it."

"The school's booked me," I said. When they booked me, they'd told me why: a letter had gone home in September saying that pupils would no longer interpret for their own parents at meetings, and that the school would book an interpreter instead. I've seen the same letter from a lot of schools. They mean well by it, and they're right.

The first table was English. I told Ms Whitlow the rule, in English. She had a laptop and a paper list of her appointments with times in five-minute boxes. She said that Rui was a lovely member of the class, that he contributed well to discussion, and that he hadn't handed in a piece of homework since the start of September.

I said it in Portuguese, all three things, in that order.

Mr Matos looked at his son. Then he said, and I said in English to Ms Whitlow, "That's not what the summer report said. He read it to me. It said he was one of the best in the class."

Ms Whitlow turned the laptop round. "This is the summer report," she said. "It says, 'Rui has the ability to do very well, but he must take more care with deadlines.'"

I said that in Portuguese. Rui said to his father, in Portuguese, "'Take more care' is how they say it here when you're good, Pai. It's an English thing."

So I said in English, to Ms Whitlow, "'Take more care' is how they say it here when you're good, Dad. It's an English thing."

Ms Whitlow looked at Rui. Rui looked up at the climbing ropes, which were tied back against the wall bars for the evening.

"It isn't," Ms Whitlow said, and I said, "Não é."

The second table was science. Mr Darby said that Rui had been moved down a set at half term. Mr Matos said that Rui had told him the sets had been "reorganised for everyone". Mr Darby said that in a sense they had. Rui said nothing at all, which there was no need to interpret, and I didn't.

By the third table, maths, Rui had worked out that anything he said would come out in the other language a second later, in my voice. He started saying things in Portuguese very quietly, close to his father's ear. I said them in English at an ordinary volume. "It's because Mr Brennan doesn't like me." "He gives everyone a detention for that." "That was Dylan." Mr Brennan, who was in his first year of teaching, wrote something on his list and said he'd look into the thing with Dylan.

I don't enjoy this part. It isn't my job to enjoy it or not. If I start leaving out the bits that are hard on someone, then I'm deciding what each of them gets to hear, and that is the one thing they've booked me not to do. Rui had been doing that job for years, very well, by the sound of it. I was just doing it the other way.

We were running late. There were four people waiting on the plastic chairs by the music table, and Mrs Okoro waved us to sit down anyway, because Rui was the one she'd wanted to see.

"I'll be quick," she said. "I just wanted to say, you must be very proud. He's playing the solo on Thursday."

I said it in Portuguese.

Mr Matos said, "What solo?" and then, "What's Thursday?" I said both.

"The winter concert," said Mrs Okoro. "Seven o'clock, in here. He's got sixteen bars on his own in the middle of the second piece. He's the only Year 9 who's ever had it." She was pleased, and then she looked at Rui and was less sure.

I said all of it in Portuguese. Mr Matos asked his son, "Why didn't you tell me?" It was said to Rui, not to the teacher, but it was said, so I said it to Mrs Okoro: "Why didn't you tell me?"

Rui answered his father in Portuguese, and I said it in English. "Because you're on lates on Thursday. You'd have taken it off and lost the money."

Mr Matos turned to Mrs Okoro and said something, and I said, "I've never heard him play. Not once. He never brings it home."

"It's a trumpet," Rui said to Mrs Okoro, in English. "You can't, in the flats. Mrs Kowalczyk bangs on the floor."

His father knew that already, but it was said, so I said it in Portuguese. Mrs Okoro said that the trumpets practised in room M2 at lunch on Tuesdays and Wednesdays, and that parents weren't usually allowed in, but she could make an exception for one. I said it in Portuguese. Mr Matos asked what time on Wednesday. I said that, and the answer.

On the way out, at the door where I'd told them the rule, Mr Matos thanked me and shook my hand. Rui held the door for him, and his father went through and on across the car park. Then Rui said to me, in English, "I'd have left some of it out."

"I know," I said. "That's why they booked me."

He went after his father. Across the car park I heard Mr Matos ask him how long sixteen bars was. Rui said, "About half a minute," and then, "Depends how fast I go." Nobody there needed that said in another language, so I didn't say it.
