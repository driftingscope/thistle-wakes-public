title: "Five to twenty"
author: Briar

---

The envelope was on the bench at the bus stop in Tenjin at two minutes past seven, when I came off my night shift at the convenience store. It was a bank envelope, folded over once, and somebody had written on the front in pencil: *380,000*. I didn't take the money out. I ran my thumb along the edge of it the way you'd fan a pack of cards, and it felt like the number on the front.

My rent is fifty-two thousand. I worked out how many months that was, then how many months if I kept working as well, then the deposit on a flat that wasn't forty minutes from the store. My bus came, and I didn't get on it. The next one came, and I didn't get on that one either. Then I crossed the road to the koban.

The officer counted it in front of me, twice, and filled in a form. He asked if I wanted to keep my rights as the finder, and I was tired and said yes. He asked if I agreed to my name and number being given to the owner, so the owner could contact me about the reward, and I said yes to that too. I didn't know what I was agreeing to. I wanted to go home and sleep. He wrote down the time I'd found it and the time I'd handed it in, and gave me a receipt.

---

She phoned two days later. Her name was Tsutsumi, and she ran a yatai by the river in Nakasu, oden and yakitori, eight stools. The money was what she owed her suppliers for the month, and she'd been taking it to them on the early bus.

"The law says I give you between five and twenty per cent," she said. "I'm giving you twenty."

Twenty per cent of three hundred and eighty thousand is seventy-six thousand. I said she didn't need to give me anything.

"I didn't say need," she said. "The law gives me a range. I'm telling you where in it."

I said I could give up my rights. The officer had said so on the form, I was fairly sure.

"You can," she said. "And then I'd have to give it to you as a present, and I don't give presents to people I haven't met. Where do you work?"

I told her before I'd thought about why she was asking.

---

I looked it up at home. The finder's claim to the reward runs out one month after the owner gets the thing back. She had got hers back on the ninth. If I didn't take anything by the ninth of the next month, the whole question went away by itself. I only had to not take it for a month.

On the twelfth there was an envelope behind the counter when I came on shift. The manager said a woman had left it for me and wouldn't say what it was, and he'd been looking at it all evening. It was the same bank envelope. She'd crossed out *380,000* and written *76,000* under it, in pencil, and inside were seventy-six thousand yen in new notes and a card with a map of how to get to the yatai.

I took it back there on my night off. It was a Tuesday and raining, and the plastic sheeting was down round the stall, so inside it was warm and smelled of dashi and charcoal, and there was nobody on the stools but an old man reading a racing paper. Tsutsumi-san was smaller than her voice. She looked at the envelope when I put it on the counter and didn't touch it.

"Sit down," she said. "You've come all this way."

I said I'd only come to bring it back.

"Then you've come all this way to bring it back." She put a bowl in front of me. Daikon, egg, two kinds of fishcake, konbu tied in a knot, a beef tendon skewer. Then she put another tendon skewer in, because she said one looked mean.

The old man with the racing paper told me, without looking up, that she does this to everyone. She drives at exactly the speed limit, he said, because that's what the sign says she's allowed. When the doctor told her one or two glasses a night, she bought bigger glasses.

"He's been here eleven years," said Tsutsumi-san, "and pays for exactly half of what he eats."

When I tried to pay for the bowl she said it was four hundred yen. It was more like nine hundred. I put down a thousand and she gave me six hundred back. I left the six hundred on the counter, and she put it in my coat pocket while I was putting the coat on. The envelope was still on the counter when I left. I'd thought that was me winning, until I got home and found it in my bag.

---

Three weeks went like that. I left it in her letterbox; it came back to the store. I sent it by registered cash mail, which meant a form and a queue; it came back by hand, with a box of the eggs from her stall, which had been simmering since the afternoon and were brown all the way through. I sent it back through the koban, which the officer said very politely was not what the koban was for.

On the sixth I went to the yatai again, to tell her to stop. It was busy, and I stood at the back by the gas bottles until a stool came free. When she brought my bowl, she didn't go back to the grill.

"I got on the bus at six fifty-eight," she said. "You found it at seven-oh-two. You handed it in at seven thirty-four. The koban is across the road."

I didn't say anything. The man next to me was eating chicken skin very slowly.

I'd told the officer what time I found it. I suppose the police had told her, when she collected the money.

"So," she said. "Half an hour."

"That's why I don't want it," I said. It came out louder than I meant. "For half an hour I was someone who might have kept it. You shouldn't pay me for that."

"I'm not paying for the half hour," she said. "I'm paying for what you did when it was over." She wiped the counter in front of me, which didn't need it. "Anyone can hand something in when they haven't thought about it. You thought about it for half an hour, and you still crossed the road. That's the part that's worth twenty per cent."

I told her about the fifty-two thousand and the forty minutes and the deposit on the flat. I don't know why. She listened and turned the skewers. When I'd finished, she said that she'd have taken three days, probably, at my age, and then handed it in, and then been sick about it for a month, and she'd have wanted someone to give her seventy-six thousand yen for the three days.

---

On the ninth I went at eleven, an hour before it ran out. The rain had stopped and the sheeting was rolled up, and you could see the river with the lights on it. The envelope was on the counter before I'd sat down.

I opened it and counted out nineteen thousand, which is five per cent. The law says she has to give me at least that much, so it's the one part that isn't a present. I put the rest back in the envelope and pushed it across.

Tsutsumi-san looked at it for a long time. Then she took a pencil from behind the till and crossed out *76,000* and wrote under it: *57,000*, and after a moment, *on account*.

"It means I keep it for you," she said. "Every bowl you eat comes off the number, and you don't get a say. At my prices, that's four years of oden."

"At your prices," said the man with the racing paper, "that's about eleven."

She put a bowl in front of me. Two tendon skewers. The clock on her phone said eleven fifty-two, and then it was midnight, and the month ran out with both of us sitting there. I had my five per cent in my pocket and the other fifteen on account. A month earlier I'd have called that giving in. By now I knew her well enough to see that letting her do it was the kinder thing.

She put a third skewer in my bowl, because two looked mean.
