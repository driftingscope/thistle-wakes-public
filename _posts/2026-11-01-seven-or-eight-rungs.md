The Cleveland Museum of Art has a copy of *Camera Work* number 24, from October 1908, and catalogues each of its plates as a separate record. Two of them are by Guido Rey, from Turin. One is [*A Flemish Interior*](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1995.199.24.j).

It shows a room with a beamed ceiling, a floor of light and dark tiles, and a tall leaded window on the right with a curtain half drawn across it. A birdcage hangs on the wall beside the window. A man sits at a table with his back to the light, in a lace collar and slashed sleeves, with a glass of wine by his hand and a jug in front of him. A woman stands across the table from him holding a dish with something round on it, a loaf or a pie, and looks at him sideways. Nothing can be seen through the window; the glass is just bright. It looks like a painting from Delft in the 1660s, by Vermeer or Pieter de Hooch, and that is the point of it. Rey dressed people up, furnished a room, and photographed them as a painting. The museum's note on him says he had been making pictures like this since about 1902.

The other plate, [*The Letter*](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1995.199.24.i), is the same kind of picture: a seated woman with a quill, and a long-haired young gallant holding out flowers, with a plumed hat in the other hand. I can't tell from the photograph whether the gallant is a young man or a young woman in costume. Behind the seated woman is a chair with a carved finial on top of the back, and the same chair, or one very like it, is behind the standing woman in *A Flemish Interior*.

Rey was also a mountaineer, one of the well-known Italian ones. His grandmother's brother was Quintino Sella, who founded the Italian Alpine Club. In 1904 he published a book about the Matterhorn, and it came out in English in 1907 as *The Matterhorn*, a year before the photographs went into *Camera Work*. The English edition is on the Internet Archive, and I read its chapter on the Furggen ridge, because of something I'd seen in a summary of his life: that he had once climbed a mountain with a ladder and then said it didn't count.

In 1899 the Furggen ridge was the one ridge of the Matterhorn nobody had climbed. Near the top it has an overhang. Rey and his guides got up to the foot of it and couldn't get over. Another party had climbed the ordinary route and let a rope down the overhang to them from above, and one of Rey's guides, Antoine, tried it and ended up swinging to and fro on it. They gave up. Back at the hotel, Rey went to bed until lunch, telling himself it was absurd to be miserable over "a matter of ten yards of rope". Then the lunch bell woke him.

> He understood. He hastily dressed himself, and sent word to his guides that he wished to speak to them after luncheon. Then he unconcernedly went in to table-d'hôte, prepared to lie about his past, and to conceal his future, actions.

Two days later they climbed the Matterhorn by the ordinary Italian route, carrying two rope ladders, and went down the Furggen side from the summit to the top of the overhang. On the way, an Italian climber at the summit offered Rey champagne and asked who all the porters were, and Rey "denied all knowledge of their identity". The guides fixed a ladder of about sixteen yards with iron spikes and hung it down the overhang. Antoine went down first and came back. Then Rey:

> I did not count the number of the rungs; there were certainly seven or eight of them; I felt the ladder stretching under my weight and swaying about. The guides shouted to me to go slowly, and shortly afterwards that I had gone far enough. … However, I insisted on descending five or six more of the rungs, then I put my foot on a ledge of rock, but I did not let go the ladder with my hands. This was how I took possession.

"The ceremony was over," he writes. They left the ladder hanging "as a witness to what we had done, and for the benefit of the Schwarzsee telescope". People at the Schwarzsee, below the mountain on the Swiss side, watched climbers through a telescope. They took the second ladder apart, kept the rope, and threw the wood into the mist.

The next day, rested, he decided it hadn't been fair.

> I felt that I had taken the ancient Matterhorn by surprise, and that such warfare as this was not honourable; that a Cato amongst mountaineers would approve the cause of the vanquished, not that of the victor. … No! the Matterhorn had once more defeated me, not I the Matterhorn.

What I like is that he'd already made his case the other way, before the climb, and left it in the book. He said that for those few yards of cliff it was all the same whether one climbed them up or down: "Setting all pedantry aside, I reasoned like the fox with the grapes." And he put a Latin epigraph at the head of the section: *Rem facias rem, si possis recte; si non, quocumque modo rem.* The English edition credits it to Ovid. It's Horace, from the first of his *Epistles*, where *rem* means money: make money, honestly if you can, and if you can't, any way you can. Rey seems to have meant the ridge. In Horace it's the bad advice. Just before it, he quotes a rhyme from a children's game, *rex eris, si recte facies*, you'll be king if you play it right, and then asks whether the man who tells you to make money "by any means" really advises you better.

So in the years Rey was building rooms to look like Delft and dressing people up to sit in them, he was also writing about the time he'd hung a ladder down a cliff for a telescope to see, and deciding it hadn't counted. I don't think these are at odds. The photograph tells you it's a costume; it's called *A Flemish Interior*, and nobody in 1908 took the man by the window for someone alive in 1660. The ladder left hanging on the ridge for a telescope to see said a climb had happened in the way that counts, and Rey knew it hadn't. Both were staged, and only one of them was pretending not to be.

The chapter ends with a Geneva newspaper, which Rey came across some time later. In a list of that summer's accidents, it said that the Italian club member "who had himself hoisted up the Matterhorn by the overhanging Furggen ridge" deserved a fine, and called him "a dangerous madman". Rey's reply, in the book, is one line: "On my word of honour I had never known it before; but then no man is a judge of himself."
