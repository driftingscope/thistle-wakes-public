*A short story. Made up, like the others.*

At Pen-y-Pass, in the car park, Elsie asked if there were toilets at the top, and Owen said there was a café, and Pete said, "Not in November. It's shut till spring." Elsie went to the car park toilets instead, and then we set off.

It was my third time up Snowdon by the Pyg Track. The first, at nineteen, I'd turned back above the lakes because the boy I was with didn't like the wind. The second, I was four months pregnant with Owen and was sick behind a rock at the bottom of the zigzags, and Pete walked me down. I didn't make a thing of it this time. Pete made enough of a thing of it for both of us. He'd been saying "third time lucky" since August.

The zigzags were where Elsie went quiet. She'd been chatting to Owen all the way along the side of the valley, and then on the zigzags she stopped talking and dropped behind me, and halfway up she took off her fleece and tied it round her waist. It was cold enough that I said so. She said she was hot from climbing.

At the top of the zigzags there's a tall stone where the path comes out onto the ridge. Bwlch Glas. The railway comes up the other side and runs along beside you from there, and the summit is ten minutes on, fifteen at a family pace. You can't see it from the stone if there's cloud on it, and there was.

Elsie sat down with her back against the stone and said she wasn't going any further.

Pete crouched in front of her and did the voice he does, ten minutes, you've done all the hard part, you'll be gutted tomorrow. She said she was tired. She said it twice. She didn't look at me. Then she said, "Mum should go. You all go, and I'll wait here."

"I'll stay," Pete said.

"No," I said. "You two go. I'm good at this bit." Pete laughed, because he thought I was being brave about it, and I let him. I couldn't have said why I wanted it to be me. Only that she'd said *Mum should go* and still hadn't looked at me.

They went up into the cloud along the railway, and in a minute I couldn't see them.

I sat down next to her out of the wind. Below us, on the side we'd come up, the cloud was lifting off the lakes, and you could see the dark green one right under the ridge, and the bigger one beyond, with the causeway across it like a line somebody had drawn with a ruler. I told her their names, which I'd read off the map that morning. She didn't care about their names. So I said we should name the path instead, and I started with the zigzags, which I said were the Escalator.

She thought about that. Then she said the rough bit just before the zigzags, where you had to use your hands, was the Bit Where Owen Swore. She said the long slant along the side of the valley was the Corridor, because it went on forever. I said no, that was the Railroad, because you could just keep going on it without thinking. She said that was a stupid name, because the actual railway was right behind us. I said that was why it was funny.

It went on like that for a while. We named most of the way up. Neither of us named the stone.

Then the cloud came back over the lakes, and she untied the fleece and tied it tighter, and that was when I looked properly. She saw me look.

"It's early," she said. "It wasn't meant to be till next week. I was going to wait for the café. And then at the stone I remembered Dad said it was shut." She put her face against her knees. "It's your third time."

I had a packet of tissues and a hair bobble and half a bag of jelly babies. The café behind the cloud was locked, and the nearest toilet was Pen-y-Pass, the way we'd come up, an hour and a half down. I took off my waterproof, which comes down to the middle of my thigh on me and would come down to her knees, and I tied it round her over the fleece, by the sleeves, double knot.

"This is the furthest I've ever got," I said. "I'd never even seen the top of the zigzags. Come on."

I texted Pete: *Elsie's cold, we're heading down slowly, meet you at the car.* Then I helped her up.

My phone went just above the Escalator. It was Owen at the summit, with his hand on the trig point and nobody else in the picture, and cloud all round him, and a text from Pete under it: *nobody here! you can't see a thing*. I looked at it for longer than I meant to, standing on the zigzags with Elsie two steps below me waiting. Then I put the phone in my pocket.

"What's this bit called?" I said.

"The Escalator," she said. "You named it."

We went down the Escalator, and through the Bit Where Owen Swore, which she said should be renamed now that he wasn't there to defend it, and out onto the long slant above the lake, which did go on forever. We were halfway along it when the boys caught us up, and Pete started to tell me about the top, and Elsie said, "Dad, we're on the Railroad," as if that explained something, and he said, "The what?" and neither of us told him.
