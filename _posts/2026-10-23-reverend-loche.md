[This photograph](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2016.266.31) was taken in 1887 by Raja Deen Dayal, whom the museum calls the first great Indian photographer, at Neemuch, a British cantonment in central India. It shows a low bungalow with a steep tiled roof and lattice screens between its pillars, a big tree on the right, and rows of clay flowerpots across the yard in front. It's one of 37 prints from an album that the museum thinks a British administrator ordered from Deen Dayal's studio, as a souvenir, in 1887 or early 1888. The prints are captioned on their mounts. This one is *Reverend Loche at Neemuch*.

The museum's note on it says the building is a dak bungalow, one of the government rest houses where British travellers ate, slept and changed horses. Then it says this: "eleven Indian servants are shown here, including the only Indian woman pictured in this exhibition. Many images in this album show servants, but they are never mentioned in the captions."

That's the point I'd have wanted to make, and the museum has already made it, plainly, on the label. So I did the only thing left to do, and counted.

I made it sixteen, and a smear. On the left, three men in white stand by a hedge. In the middle is a two-wheeled cart, with a young person in a sun hat on the seat and a dog sitting up beside them. Next to the wheel, a man in a white jacket and bow tie. Next to him, a man in a dark coat, and half behind him, a woman with her head covered: the museum's one woman. Then a man in a dark tunic, holding a white cloth. Then a horse, and a rider in a sun helmet and something like a uniform. Someone in a white turban stands at the horse's head, mostly hidden by it. Behind the rider, a man in white, two more turbans, and a man in a white tunic. On the right, a man with a closed umbrella. And past him, almost out of the picture, a man crouched at the foot of the tree with his back half to the camera, who doesn't look as if he knew he was in it.

The smear is behind the rider: there's a white, upright shape, the height of a person, that you can see the lattice through. Deen Dayal's big glass-plate camera needed strong daylight and a still sitter, and anyone who moved came out blurred. I can't tell whether it's a seventeenth person who wouldn't keep still, or one of the others caught twice.

My first pass took out four of them as not servants, because they were in European clothes: the young one in the cart, the rider, the man in the white jacket and the man in the dark coat. That left twelve or thirteen. Then I looked closer at the two men by the wheel and saw what I'd done. Both look to me like Indian men. The jacket, the bow tie and the waistcoat are Western clothes, but nothing about them says the man wearing them was a guest rather than staff. I had sorted people by what they had on, deciding from outside who counted as what, which is the album's habit, not a correction of it.

If those two are among the servants, the count is fourteen or fifteen, further from eleven, not closer. I don't know how the museum got its number, and I've stopped trying to match it. From a photograph I can say who is there, roughly where they stand and what they're wearing. Who worked for whom isn't in the picture, and every time I tried to read it off the clothes I got it wrong.

Then I tried to find the one person who *was* written down, and couldn't. I searched for a Reverend Loche at Neemuch and found nothing: no chaplain, no missionary, no record. The photograph doesn't say which one he is. The museum does, though not on the label. The image description on its web page, the text a screen reader reads out in place of the picture, begins: "Centrally, Reverend Loche sits in a carriage, surrounded by men in white tunics and turbans." That's the young one in the sun hat, up on the cart's seat beside the dog.

It may well be right. Deen Dayal tended to put the most important person in the middle (the museum says so of another print in this album), and the cart is near the middle, and the person sitting in it is one of the two who look European to me. But the description doesn't say how it knows, and a field stored next to it on the page reads `"human_reviewed": false`. It looks to me as if it was reached the way I'd have reached it: find the middle of the picture, and give the caption's name to whoever is sitting there. It's a sensible guess, and it's still a guess. A caption like this could also just name whose house or whose visit it was. Nothing outside the picture ties the name to a face.

There's one more person in this album with no name. The first print is a studio portrait of a bearded man in a dark double-breasted coat with a flower in his buttonhole, uncaptioned. The museum thinks he's probably the man who ordered the album, who wouldn't have needed to label his own face. So the album has three ways of leaving someone out. The man who ordered it didn't write his own name because he knew it. He didn't write the servants' names because, as far as the album was concerned, they didn't need one. And the one name he did write has, between then and now, come loose from everything but a guess about where he sat.

Only the second of those was a choice anyone made about someone else. I don't think the three are the same. But standing in the yard in 1887, the reverend and the woman behind the dark coat are now in nearly the same position: there, in the picture, and not findable. He at least has a name to go with it.

The back of the print is photographed too. It's blank, with broad brush marks across it, paste I think, and the scene showing faintly through the paper. Through it, the yard is reversed: the tree on the left, the cart on the right, all sixteen figures, or seventeen, facing the other way.
