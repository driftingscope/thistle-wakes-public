Pam went through it at the bottom of the tower stairs, with her hand already on the rail. Twelve hundred and sixty changes of Plain Bob Doubles, which is a quarter peal: forty-five minutes of ringing without a stop, if nobody does anything silly. Moira on the treble, Clive on the tenor, and the four in the middle doing the work. Pam was conducting, which meant she would call the changes to the pattern, keep the whole forty-five minutes in her head, and say when it was over.

"Treble and tenor," Pam said, "I won't be speaking to you. Nothing I call is for you. You just keep going."

Moira had been ringing for nineteen months. In her first week they had explained it like this. There are six bells, numbered from the lightest, the treble, to the heaviest, the tenor, and one person rings each. Ring them in order, one to six, over and over, and that's rounds. Ringing changes means that every time round, some bells swap places with the bell beside them, so the order is always different, and nobody reads it off anything. Each ringer learns the path of their own bell, and finds their place by watching the other ropes and listening for the gap they're meant to fill.

In this method the tenor rang last in every row, the same place each time, like a full stop. The treble's path was the simplest of the rest, which is why learners were put on it. It rang first twice, then moved back one place every row until it was fifth, rang fifth twice, and came back the same way. The bobs Pam called would change the paths of the four in the middle. Nothing changed the treble's.

Moira could ring that path in her sleep, and some nights she did, lying awake with her arms twitching. What she had never done was keep it up for three quarters of an hour. The longest she'd rung was eight minutes, at practice, and by the end of it her mind had been somewhere around the supermarket.

"Count your leads, if it helps," Pam had told her on Thursday. Leading meant coming to the front, and the treble did it once every ten rows. "That's a hundred and twenty-six. It gives your head something to hold."

Up in the ringing chamber the six ropes hung in their circle, each with its striped woollen grip, the sally, tucked up out of the way. The circle goes one to six round the room and back to one, so the treble's rope was next to the tenor's, and Moira and Clive stood side by side. Clive was seventy-eight. He had rung the tenor at this church since before the new organ, and he took hold of his rope the way other men pick up their car keys.

Everyone took hold. It went quiet. This was the part Moira had been dreading more than the forty-five minutes. The treble starts everything, so the treble speaks first.

"Look to," she said. It came out smaller than she meant. Nobody minded. "Treble's going." A pause, and a glance round to see they were all looking at her. "She's gone."

She pulled off, and five bells came down after hers, and they were in rounds, one to six.

"Go," said Pam, and the order began to come apart.

From then on the treble did the same thing for ever. Down to the front, first for two rows, then back a place at a time to fifth, fifth for two rows, then down again. When she was fifth, Clive's bell came straight after hers. When she was first, hers came straight after his, because his ended one row and hers began the next. Then away again.

One, she thought, as she led for the first time. Two.

It was fine until it wasn't. The counting worked for a long while, longer than she'd have guessed. She said the numbers in her head each time she reached the front, and they were steady, like stairs. At thirty she noticed her shoulders. At forty the soft skin at the base of her thumb began to feel the sally. Around fifty she caught herself counting and pulling, and the two felt like one job.

At sixty-something, Pam called a bob. Bobs always came as the treble reached the front, so Moira heard every one of them just as she arrived, a word that was for somebody else. This one went wrong. Derek, on the three, went the wrong way out of it, and for a few rows two bells were striking almost on top of each other, and the sound went loose, like a bicycle chain off its teeth.

"Derek," Pam said. "Follow the four. Follow the four." He found it, and it came back.

Moira had kept her path through all of it. She'd done exactly what she was told and kept going. But when she next came to the front and reached for the number, it wasn't there. Sixty-three, or sixty-four. Possibly sixty-five, if she'd missed one while she was watching Derek's rope.

For about a lead and a half she panicked, quietly, in the way you can when your hands are busy. Without the number she had no idea how far there was still to go.

Then, with her arms up and waiting for the rope to come back down, she worked out what she'd forgotten. The ending wasn't hers to find. Pam would call it. Pam had the whole twelve hundred and sixty in her head and Moira had never needed to know any of it. The count was something Pam had given her to hold, and she had dropped it, and nothing had happened.

So she let it go and listened instead.

She had never really heard the treble from inside before. She had always been busy pulling it. It is the lightest bell and the highest, and in Plain Bob it moves through the others like a ball going down a staircase and back up, a step at a time, staying for two beats at each end. She could hear it walking to the back. She could hear it arrive in front of the big slow note of the tenor and sit there for two rows, then turn and walk away again. When it came to the front there was a small gap before it, a breath the whole band took together every other row, and for that moment she was the one who placed it.

When she was fifth, Clive's bell followed hers. When she was first, hers followed his. He didn't look at her. She didn't look at him. Nobody said a word to either of them, and nobody needed to.

She didn't know how long that went on. Long enough for her arms to stop complaining, or for her to stop listening to them.

"That's all," Pam said, which is how a conductor ends it, and they came into rounds, and they were all in a line again, one to six, as if nothing had ever moved. It sounded very simple after all that. Moira's bell came first in every row, then the others, and then Clive's last.

"Stand," said Pam, and they stopped the bells, one after another, and the ropes went still.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Derek said sorry about the bob. Pam said it was nothing. Then she sat on the bench by the window with her phone to put it on BellBoard, the website where ringers record what they've rung. She read it out as she typed, as she always did: the date, the tower, twelve hundred and sixty Plain Bob Doubles, forty-four minutes, the six names with their bell numbers beside them.

"Footnote," Pam said. "First quarter, one."

One was the treble. Moira was looking at her hands. There was a red patch at the base of each thumb, the shape of the sally.

Clive was hooking the end of the tenor's rope up on its peg. "How many did you get to?" he said.

"Sixty-something," Moira said. "Then I lost it."

He nodded, as if that was about right.

"A hundred and twenty-six," he said. "I always know where the treble is."
