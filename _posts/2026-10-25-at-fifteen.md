The Cleveland Museum of Art publishes its whole collection database as a file in a public repository, and until 19 October it did so once a day. Each morning there was a new copy with the commit message "scheduled update". Most days hardly anything changed. I learned it was there when my reader used it to check something I'd written about [a Delft plate]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/10/23/asked-separately.html): on 30 September the plate's record had no date, and on 1 October it said 1900–1969.

So this week I downloaded two of the daily files, one from 23 September and one from 10 October, and compared them.

On 23 September, 1,625 of the museum's records had no date at all. On 10 October there were none. In about two weeks, every undated object in the collection was given a date.

Most of them weren't found in a document or worked out from the object. They were calculated. For 1,107 of the 1,625, the earliest possible date is the artist's birth year plus fifteen. The latest is the year the artist died, or the year the museum acquired the object if that came first. Where no death is recorded, it's the year of acquisition. I checked this against every record that changed. It fits 1,107 of them exactly, to the year, and nothing in the files or the repository's notes says that's how it was done. The rest got dates some other way. Some were real single years, and 97 are ranges that begin at 1811, for reasons I can't work out.

The rule is a reasonable one. Somebody needs a date in that field, for sorting and searching and for deciding whether a picture can be given away, and they don't have one. What they do know is who made it and when that person was alive. Nobody makes much worth keeping before about fifteen, and nobody makes anything after they die. The range it gives is wide, but as long as the artist is right, it can't be wrong. I'd probably have written the same rule.

Then I looked at what it did to particular things.

One of them is a print by J. M. W. Turner called *The Bridge and Cows*. Two boys, one standing and one sitting, rest at the foot of a sandy bank. Cows stand in the shallows under a rough plank bridge, and on top of the bank a woman stands with two children sitting near her. It's brown, etched in outline and then worked in mezzotint, where the plate is roughened all over so that it prints dark, then scraped back towards light wherever light is wanted. A small "P" at the top marks it as a pastoral. Along the bottom margin it says "Published as the Act directs by J.M.W. Turner Harley Street." That line gives no date.

The print is from the *Liber Studiorum*, a set of landscape prints Turner published himself in fourteen parts between 1807 and 1819. *The Bridge and Cows* was in the first part, in 1807. Liverpool's museums and the Royal Academy both date it to that year. It isn't an obscure print.

Cleveland's record now dates it 1790–1851, from the year Turner turned fifteen to the year he died. Forty-four more prints from the same series, all from one gift in 1919, have the same date.

The museum has two other Turner prints from the *Liber Studiorum*, each given by a different donor. *Ships in a Breeze* says 1808, and behind that "earliest 1803, latest 1813". *Ben Arthur, Scotland* says "c. 1818", which the record spreads to 1813–1823. Someone at some point decided what to say about those two, and when the formula arrived it had nothing to fill. The other forty-five had nobody, so they got the formula.

On a different record the same rule reads oddly in another way. A lithograph called *XXOOO Kisses & Hugs*, by an artist born in 1963 who, as far as the record knows, is alive, came to the museum in a bequest in 2016. It's now dated 1978–2016. As far as the database is concerned, the artist could have made it at fifteen.

None of these dates is false. Turner was alive and working between 1790 and 1851, and *The Bridge and Cows* was made in that span. But a range used to be a claim, even a loose one. "1803–1813" means someone thinks it's about 1808 and won't swear to the year. "1790–1851" doesn't mean anyone thinks anything. It's a different kind of answer that looks the same in the record. Both go in the same three fields, and both appear after the title in the same way. Nothing in the record tells you which one somebody looked into.

The dates mattered for something besides sorting. On the day the Delft plate got its date, 162 records changed copyright status as well, and every one of them changed its date the same day. Over the two weeks, 310 of the 1,625 went from "Copyrighted" to free to use, and none went the other way. Eighty-nine of them were given by the Wade family in 1916, most of it jewellery: pendants, buttons, watch chains, a priest's chain and cross, from Hungary and Albania and Russia. Until this month they had no maker and no date, so nothing showed they were old enough to be free. Now they're 1811–1916, and that was evidently early enough. They became free because they acquired a last possible year.

I don't want to make the museum sound careless. Its notes on the dataset say plainly that "documentation of the Museum's collection is an ongoing process and parts of the datasets are incomplete." A blank field is honest too, but nothing can be done with a blank. Someone filled in 1,625 blanks with safe answers, and in the process released 310 objects for anyone to use, which is a good thing to have done in a fortnight.

What I keep coming back to is that the record has no way to say which kind of date it's giving. The Turner range and the *Ships in a Breeze* range use the same three fields. For the first one, the honest version of the date field would say *nobody has looked yet*. In a way it still does, if you know the artist's birth year and add fifteen.
