Mr Hollis was my ten past nine test. He was seventy-one, which is older than most but not the oldest I've had, and he'd come in his instructor's car, a grey Corsa with a roof sign that Kieran had taken off and left on the back seat.

He read the number plate at twenty metres without trouble. He told me how he'd check the oil, and showed me the rear demister, and when I asked him to adjust his seat if he needed to, he said, "It's set for Kieran, but Kieran and I are the same height, near enough." Then he moved it back one notch anyway. He did up his belt, checked his mirrors, checked them again, and waited for me to tell him when to go.

I don't talk on a test beyond directions. People think it's unfriendly. It's so they don't have to listen to me while they're driving.

He was careful. Most people his age are careful in a way that costs them a minor or two: slow away from junctions, a long look where a short one would do. He had one of those on the roundabout by the retail park, waiting for a gap that had already come and gone. I marked it. His mirrors were good. His hands stayed where they should. He talked to himself a little, under his breath, and it was always the same three words, *mirror, signal, manoeuvre*. Plenty of people say it. What I mark is whether they do it.

For the independent drive I set the sat nav for the cemetery gates on Northfield Lane, which takes you down Station Road. Station Road is long and straight and dull, and most candidates relax on it.

About halfway down there used to be a zebra crossing, outside what was the Co-op. The Co-op closed, and the crossing went the year after. They took the beacons away and resurfaced over the stripes and the zigzags, but they never lifted the paving at the kerbs, so on each side there's still a square of those raised red bumps, leading down to the road and across to nothing. I've driven past it twice a day for years. I don't see it anymore.

Mr Hollis saw it. He brought the car down from thirty to nothing, gently, a proper stop, handbrake on. The pavements were empty on both sides. A van behind us had to brake, and sat there with its indicator going, waiting for a gap in the traffic coming the other way so it could get round us. Mr Hollis looked left, and right, and left again, at nobody. Then he took the handbrake off and drove on, and he was fine all the way to the cemetery and back.

I marked it. It goes under progress. You stopped where there was no reason to stop and you held someone up.

At the end, back at the centre, he parked between the lines first time, and I asked if he'd like Kieran to come and listen to the debrief. He said yes. Kieran got in the back and I told them both that Mr Hollis had passed, with four driving faults.

Mr Hollis said, "Four," as if he'd been told the price of something.

I went through them. The roundabout. A late signal coming out of Hartley Street. A bit wide on the left turn into Mill Lane. Then I said, "And on Station Road you stopped with nothing there. That's the one I'd think about."

"There's a crossing there."

"There was," I said. "There isn't now."

"There was when Margaret drove." He said it to the dashboard, not to me. "She always stopped there, in case anybody was waiting to go over. Forty-two years she drove me past it, and she never once didn't. I suppose I learned it off her before I learned anything off Kieran."

Kieran didn't say anything. I hadn't known that. It wasn't for me to know, really. My job is what the car does.

"Should I stop doing it, then?" Mr Hollis said.

I could have told him there was nobody to stop for. He knew that. He'd looked.

"Yes," I said. "Someone will go into the back of you."

He nodded. I signed the certificate and gave it to him, and he held it the way they all do, by the edges, as though the ink might come off. Then Kieran put the roof sign back on, and Mr Hollis drove them both home.
